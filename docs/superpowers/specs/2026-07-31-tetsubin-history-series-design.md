# Design spec — Tetsubin history and regional traditions series

**Date:** 2026-07-31

**Type:** Six-part illustrated series for the tea collection

**Status:** Approved design, pending implementation plan
**Collection:** `src/content/tea/`

## 1. Purpose

Create a factual, readable, heavily illustrated series about the history of the Japanese iron kettle and the traditions that shaped it. The series begins with the four HOJO articles supplied by the author, but does not reproduce their structure or depend on them alone. It checks their claims against museum catalogues, Japanese government and craft-association material, curator-supervised histories, official workshop records, and specialist object research.

The series must explain both the shared craft and the real differences between regions and workshops. It must also give readers practical identification help through documented examples of maker marks, without turning uncertain attribution into false certainty.

## 2. Editorial thesis

The history of Japanese iron casting is older than the tetsubin. The tetsubin is best understood as an Edo-period adaptation associated with the spread of sencha and everyday tea drinking, not simply as a sixteenth-century chanoyu utensil. Its regional traditions overlap: Morioka and Mizusawa have separate histories under the modern Nambu name; Yamagata developed its own thin-wall casting language; Kyoto and Kansai made the kettle into elaborate art metal; and Takaoka now reinterprets parts of that Kyoto vocabulary through a specialized network of casting, engraving, coloring, and inlay workshops.

Regional labels describe tendencies, not immutable rules. Individual workshops, generations, production grades, commissions, and later repairs can cross those boundaries.

## 3. Format and scope

Publish six linked posts. The historical introduction targets 2,500–3,500 words. Each regional post targets 3,000–4,500 words, adjusted only when the available primary evidence genuinely supports more or less. Selected producers receive equal editorial weight: approximately 600–900 words and 6–10 useful images each, including at least two documented mark examples where such images survive.

The series is not a complete directory of every current Japanese foundry. Producers are selected because they represent a distinct lineage, method, historical transition, or documented regional practice and because enough reliable material exists to write a factual profile.

### Part 1 — The birth of the tetsubin

**Working title:** *The Tetsubin Is Younger Than You Think*

**Slug:** `tetsubin-history-1-birth-of-the-iron-kettle`

Sections:

1. A kettle with an older shadow — distinguish the age of casting traditions from the age of the tetsubin.
2. What the object is — define *chagama*, *tedorikama*, tetsubin, and enamelled cast-iron kyūsu.
3. Sencha and the portable kettle — explain the probable eighteenth-century emergence while stating that no exact invention date is secure.
4. From Edo household tool to Meiji art object — domestic adoption, workshops, exhibitions, and export.
5. One country, several casting histories — introduce Morioka, Mizusawa/Ōshū, Yamagata, Kyoto/Kansai, and Takaoka on a map.
6. How a tetsubin is made — common vocabulary of outer mould, core, spout, casting, finishing, handle, lid, lacquer, and oxide treatment; regional differences are reserved for later posts.
7. How to read the rest of the series — regional tendencies, workshop variation, marks, replacement parts, and confidence levels.

Core visuals:

- Historical-object hero.
- Labelled *chagama* / tetsubin / enamelled kyūsu comparison.
- Two-track timeline: regional casting history versus tetsubin history.
- Sencha or domestic-hearth historical image.
- Japan map locating the five production histories.
- Step-by-step casting diagram using documented workshop photography as reference.
- Museum details of handle, spout, lid, interior, and underside.

## 4. Standard producer-profile template

Every selected producer receives the same sequence and comparable depth:

1. **Verified chronology** — founding, teachers or parent lineages, succession, relocations, closures, revivals, and current status. Workshop claims are attributed when independent confirmation is unavailable.
2. **How the workshop makes kettles** — mould type, casting organization, finishing, handles, lids, coloring, distinctive techniques, and the limits of available documentation.
3. **Visual language** — representative shapes, proportions, surface patterns, imagery, inlay, lid and knob choices, spouts, handle construction, and changes across generations.
4. **Representative objects** — complete, securely attributed examples with dates or broad periods, dimensions where useful, and source provenance.
5. **Maker identification** — documented marks and inscriptions, their locations and construction, variants, and attribution cautions.
6. **What makes it distinct** — a short evidence-based comparison with neighboring workshops, avoiding quality rankings and unsupported taste claims.

## 5. Maker-mark identification standard

“Stamp” is used only as reader-friendly shorthand. Each caption and description names the actual technique when known: cast mark, impressed mark, engraved inscription, chiseled signature, lid inscription, box inscription, paper label, or certificate.

Each identification section must include:

- At least two high-resolution mark examples when documented examples are available.
- A view of the complete kettle associated with each mark.
- The original characters, normalized reading, romanization, and English meaning where meaningful.
- The mark's location: base, lower body, shoulder, underside of lid, box, or label.
- The method by which it was made.
- Documented date or generation range; otherwise an explicit “range not established.”
- Side-by-side variants only when their attribution is supported by a museum, workshop, dated catalogue, securely provenanced object, or repeatable specialist reference.
- A confidence label: **documented**, **strongly attributed**, or **uncertain**.

The prose must warn readers that:

- A signed lid does not automatically authenticate the body.
- Lids, handles, boxes, and certificates may be replacements or later pairings.
- Popular historical marks have been copied.
- Workshops changed marks and sometimes produced unmarked work.
- Regional style alone cannot prove a maker.
- Authentication should combine construction, surface, form, mark, and provenance.

A final comparison plate in Part 6 collects the best-documented marks from the entire series and links back to the full profiles.

## 6. Voice and readability

Match the strongest existing tea essays:

- Direct, curious, skeptical, and comfortable correcting received wisdom.
- First-person observations used sparingly and only when they clarify how a claim was assessed.
- Short paragraphs and descriptive section titles.
- Japanese terms introduced with characters, romanization, and a plain-English definition on first use.
- Concrete objects and workshop details before abstract summaries.
- Comparison tables after the prose has explained what the categories mean.
- No generic travel writing, sales language, quality rankings, or claims that a high price proves superior craft.
- No unsupported claims that a particular iron recipe, porosity, oxidation state, or workshop reliably produces better-tasting water.

Each post opens with an `info-box` explaining the evidence and image provenance. Each ends with an annotated source list and previous/next links to the series.

## 7. Research and source hierarchy

Use sources in this order:

1. Museum records and collection catalogues for objects, dates, marks, materials, and provenance.
2. Japanese government, prefectural, municipal, and recognized craft-association sources for regional histories, designated techniques, and current organizations.
3. Curator-supervised research, exhibition catalogues, books, and academic or institutional projects.
4. Official workshop histories for succession, internal techniques, and current activity; clearly identify self-reported claims.
5. HOJO and other specialist retailers for interviews, workshop observations, comparison ideas, and images that are unavailable elsewhere. Cross-check historical, scientific, superlative, and attribution claims before using them.
6. Auction catalogues only for documented object photography or provenance that can be checked elsewhere; never use auction copy as sole proof of a lineage or date.

Starting source set:

- HOJO general tetsubin, Suzuki Morihisa, Kunzan, and Seikodo articles.
- California Academy of Sciences tetsubin research archive.
- Traditional Crafts Aoyama Square entries for Nambu ironware, Yamagata casting, and Takaoka metalwork.
- Government of Japan *Highlighting Japan* features on Nambu ironware, Iwachu, and Kikuchi Hojudo.
- Kyoto Women’s University / Iwate Museum of Art curator-supervised Nambu visual history on Google Arts & Culture.
- Nambu Tekki Association and official Iwate tourism material.
- Yamagata craft export portal entries for Seikodo and Chobundo.
- Official Suzuki Morihisa and Kunzan workshop histories.
- Princeton University Art Museum, British Museum, Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, and other museum object records.
- Royal College of Art Tetsubin Project.
- Official or institutionally documented sources for Oigen, Oitomi, Iwachu, Kamasada, Unshikido, and the selected Takaoka producers.

Known corrections that the research must preserve:

- Do not state that tetsubin are securely documented from the sixteenth century.
- Do not describe the tetsubin as the standard water vessel of formal chanoyu; distinguish it from the *chagama*.
- Suzuki Morihisa is now in its sixteenth generation, not its fifteenth.
- Do not call the thirteenth Suzuki Morihisa a Living National Treasure; use the official recognition wording.
- Use Seikodo's documented 1735 founding date rather than “the 1600s.”
- Present conflicting Ryūbundō foundation dates explicitly and prefer securely documented life dates and museum chronology over a repeated exact year.
- Do not repeat the claim that Kyoto tetsubin production has completely disappeared; cover Unshikidō's revival and state the limits of the surviving evidence.

## 8. Image strategy

Target a regional visual essay rather than a product catalogue. Each major historical or regional section should have a visual that carries information.

Image sources include:

- Public-domain or open-access museum collection images.
- Government and craft-association process photography.
- Curator-supervised historical and workshop photography.
- Official workshop photographs.
- Credited HOJO photographs where they uniquely document a workshop or process.
- Original SVG maps, timelines, silhouettes, process diagrams, mark comparison plates, and annotated details.

For every externally sourced image:

- Store a local copy under the post's asset directory; do not hotlink.
- Preserve a source ledger with original URL, creator or institution, object title, date accessed, stated rights, and required credit line.
- Use an accurate descriptive `alt` attribute and a caption that identifies the object, maker, period, view, source, and credit.
- Do not crop out marks, accession numbers, or other identification evidence.
- Prefer images with sufficient resolution to show casting and inscriptions.
- Compress only to the point that small marks remain legible.

The author has approved the use of credited HOJO and official workshop images. Where a page does not publish a reuse license, captions still credit and link the source, and the source ledger flags the permission status so the author can obtain permission before final publication if needed.

## 10. Verification and failure handling

Before claiming completion:

- Trace every exact date, succession claim, named technique, and mark attribution to the research ledger.
- Remove or qualify claims that rely only on sales copy or conflict with stronger evidence.
- Confirm every image path, caption, credit, and source-ledger entry.
- Check downloaded file types rather than trusting extensions.
- Check image dimensions and file sizes; preserve readable mark details.
- Run `npm run build` and resolve all schema, Markdown, and asset errors.
- Render every post and inspect desktop and mobile widths.
- Check tables, image galleries, long Japanese inscriptions, captions, and previous/next links for overflow and readability.
- Run a broken-link and missing-asset check across all six posts.

If a producer lacks enough reliable information or securely attributed mark images, do not fill the gap with marketplace speculation. State the evidentiary limit in the profile and use fewer examples rather than lowering the standard.

## 11. Acceptance criteria

- Six linked posts with the approved regional structure.
- Every selected producer receives comparable word count, section structure, and visual attention.
- HOJO is used and credited but is not the sole outline, source base, or image source.
- The series makes the history/casting-history distinction clear.
- Regional differences are explained as historical and technical tendencies, not rigid authentication rules.
- Each producer includes documented identification guidance and maker-mark examples where available.
- All marks carry transcription, location, method, evidence, and confidence information.
- Borrowed images are locally stored, captioned, credited, linked, and recorded in the image ledger.
- Original maps and comparison diagrams match the blog's existing restrained visual style.
- Sources are annotated and claims of differing strength are clearly distinguished.
- All six pages build and render correctly on desktop and mobile.

## 12. Out of scope

- A complete directory of every Japanese ironware company.
- Price guides, buying recommendations, investment advice, or maker rankings.
- Authentication guarantees based on a mark photograph alone.
- Restoration and care instructions beyond what is necessary to distinguish original construction from later replacement parts.
- A scientific claim that one regional tradition or iron recipe makes objectively better water.
