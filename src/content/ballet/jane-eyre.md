Staging *Jane Eyre* as a ballet is a venturesome ambition. A story told in the first person, one that contains so much inner tension and struggle, seems very hard to project through the limited language of bodily movement without changing too much of the book's original intention to show the complexity of the heroine's inner world. An ambition that is hard not to admire, but easy to expect to fail.

As I missed the premiere, I had both the time and a justification to read up on the reviews written by then, and, without much surprise, found little agreement on the success of this ambition. While most critics had a moderately positive perception of the ballet, few could agree on exactly what was good or bad. Some even expressed absolutely opposite opinions, for instance, on whether it was easy to follow the plot without knowing the book.

Having the advantage of reading the criticism before watching the ballet, I could understand where these disagreements came from.

In my experience, the ballet was far from equal in all its parts, with some sparking excitement close to religious ecstasy, while others barely allowed me to keep my eyes open...

The ballet did have a few extraordinary scenes that felt like revelations, where you suddenly feel enlightened and get that "wow" feeling. My favourite was the dance of Jane, with Bertha's shadows dancing alongside her on a red curtain, looking like dancing flames of fire — a mixture carrying so much symbolism in the story... It was magic.

Another such "wow" moment I experienced during a pas de deux. In fact, probably the strongest parts of the ballet were those where the two protagonists were dancing together, and one could feel the intensity of their emotions and the complexity of the feelings between them.

And here it is necessary to emphasise the work of Ryan Tomash, who played Edward Rochester. He managed to convey such a complex bouquet of feelings through his movements, facial expressions, and interpretation of the music that it was hard to believe it was even possible.

Another dancer who deserves to be singled out is Katherine Stevens, whom I noticed some time ago in the corps de ballet for her sensuality, and who successfully stepped into the role of young Jane Eyre entrusted to her. It is nice to see a young talent taking a step higher in the super-competitive world of ballet, and I believe she has a bright future ahead of her.

The other soloists and members of the corps de ballet also deserve applause, as they were simply good at creating, in my opinion, the atmosphere that was expected of them.

I feel much less comfortable talking about the central heroine of the show — Silvia Selvini, who played Jane Eyre… She is, without a doubt, a superb dancer, with the strong technique and musicality one would expect from a principal dancer of a company steeped in the Bournonville tradition. However, I do not think she succeeded in getting under the skin of Jane Eyre and truly living the role. "Не верю!" ("I don't believe it!") — as the legendary theatre director Konstantin Stanislavski would say.

Perhaps she was simply not made for this role, did not have enough time to understand it, or whatever the reason might be — but I believe her somewhat underwhelming performance contributed to the "downs" of the show.

I am sorry for being harsh, but a character who was supposed to be at the centre of the story was often getting lost in the shadows of other, more expressive dancers, and one could start to wonder: "Who is playing Jane Eyre here?" If not for Ryan Tomash, the show might have felt terribly dull.

And it is funny, and somewhat sarcastic, that at the end of the show, you could see both Edward and Jane standing in the spotlight, with Edward slowly disappearing into the darkness and leaving Jane alone… Well, throughout the whole show, it felt quite the opposite.

And how much I regret not being able to see the premiere! I believe Emma Riis-Kofoed as Jane and Astrid Elbo as Bertha are exactly the dancers who could have perfectly fit the bill and taken the show to a much higher level, because these two roles seem almost written for them.

Let's move to the choreography.

I will be honest: I am not the biggest fan of contemporary dance as a style, and there was too much of it for me in this ballet. However, these are my personal preferences and have nothing to do with the ballet's merits.

What I found disappointing, though, was the way it was used. *Jane Eyre* is, above all, about the inner struggles of its heroine and the contradictions between her inner and external worlds. Yet the choreography, in my perception, was often more concerned with telling the literal story of Jane Eyre than revealing her inner perceptions and struggles.

There were a few bright, perhaps even genius, sparks where all the dots connected, but they were sporadic — more like a few pearls in a pit of sand than a meticulously crafted pearl necklace.

Several critics mentioned the repetitiveness of Cathy Marston's choreography in the ballet. Even though I could understand the choreographer's intention to connect Jane's past with her present through repetitive movements — which makes perfect sense and is a brilliant idea — I cannot disagree with those critics or allow this intention to justify the feeling of boredom that sometimes took over me as I watched the same movement patterns again and again.

You surely could have connected the dots without making my neighbours snore!

All in all, it is nice to see renowned foreign choreographers on the stage of the Royal Danish Ballet. It is refreshing, different, and controversial. Perhaps not entirely perfect, but full of ambition and sparks of genius.

Exactly what is needed to move discussions about art forward and promote ballet — and exactly what could be seen in the critics' reviews.
