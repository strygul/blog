## Takaoka and Its Metalworking Tradition

Takaoka's casting industry began in 1611, when Maeda Toshinaga invited seven casters to the new castle town. They first supplied practical objects such as pots, kettles, farming tools, and temple bells. Over time, the city became especially famous for copper-alloy work: Buddhist fittings, flower vessels, sculpture, and richly decorated objects for the domestic and export markets.

That long history is important, but it is not the history of one continuous tetsubin school. The iron kettle had already developed elsewhere, and no reliable source gives 1611 as the beginning of tetsubin production in Takaoka. What the city contributed was a mature network of metalworkers who could model, cast, polish, engrave, inlay, color, and assemble a complex object.

This makes Takaoka different from the family casting lines described in Morioka or Yamagata. A modern Takaoka kettle may be coordinated by one artist or company while several specialists make its body, handle, lid, decoration, and finish. The result can resemble the art-metal kettles of Kyoto, but resemblance is not lineage. Takaoka is best understood as a contemporary regional tradition built from its own industrial strengths.

## How Takaoka Makes a Kettle

The technique most closely associated with Takaoka tetsubin is lost-wax casting. The maker models the body in wax, surrounds it with layers of refractory material, then heats the mould so the wax runs out. Molten iron fills the space left behind. A separate core keeps the body hollow and forms the interior of the spout.

Because the wax model is sacrificed, it can carry relief that would be difficult to repeat with a simple turned mould. Dragons, waves, flowers, geometric grounds, and irregular surfaces can all be built directly into the model. The mould is broken after casting, so the process suits small runs and one-off work better than mass production.

Casting is only the beginning. The rough body is cleaned and fired, then colored with heat, iron solutions, lacquer, or combinations of them. A forged-iron handle and a cast-copper or brass lid may be made separately. Gold, silver, or copper can be hammered into prepared grooves to create inlay. Takaoka's official craft profile describes this division of work across model makers, casters, finishers, polishers, engravers, inlay artists, and colorists.

Not every kettle uses every technique. A plain wax-cast body and an inlaid exhibition piece belong to the same regional system, but they should be described from their own production records rather than from Takaoka's reputation as a whole.

## Koryo Kinjudo: A Revived Takaoka Name

Koryo Kinjudo is written <code>高陵金寿堂</code>, *Kōryō Kinjūdō*. Its operator traces the earlier Kinjudo name to the Kanamori Sahei casting family. According to that account, the family made pots and *kama* during the Edo period, then produced copperware and tetsubin under the name Kinjudo in the Meiji period.

The original business closed in the early Shōwa era. About sixty years later, during the Heisei era, the present organization restored the Kinjudo name and added *Koryo*, an old name for Takaoka. That distinction matters: this is a deliberate revival of a respected local name, not a documented uninterrupted chain of premises, tools, and makers.

Koryo Kinjudo now offers copperware, silverware, and tetsubin. Its history provides useful context for the name, but it does not publish a dependable chronology of kettle marks or enough complete object records to identify an unknown antique from a seal alone.

## Ginshodo: Contemporary Kyoto-Style Utsushi

Ginshodo, written <code>銀祥堂</code>, is a current Takaoka maker and seller of metal tea utensils. Its profile names Shinobu Ōgoshi, working as *Zuishin*, as proprietor and artist. He creates designs, applies gilding, directs production, and brings together specialists from different metalworking trades. His description of “Takaoka as one workshop” is a good summary of the city's collaborative model.

The catalogue ranges from restrained iron kettles to wax-cast bodies with gold-and-silver inlay. Several titles openly use the word *utsushi* (写し): there are kettles described as copies or interpretations of Ryūbundō, Kibundō, and Zōroku forms. One example is the *Ryūbundō utsushi Arahada Hiramaru*, a low round kettle with a rough cast surface.

Here *utsushi* is useful information, not an embarrassment. It means that a contemporary maker is consciously studying or reworking an older design. It does not mean the kettle was made by Ryūbundō, descended from that workshop, or shares the provenance of a Meiji-period original. Ginshodo's own wording keeps homage and authorship separate.

For modern Ginshodo work, the named catalogue model and its paperwork are more useful than stylistic guesswork. The company does not publish a historical seal sequence that can turn an isolated mark into a secure attribution.

## Shobee: Wax Casting Through a Production Network

Shobee is the short commercial form of Shōbē (庄兵衛); current product records name the artist as Kita Shōbē (喜多庄兵衛). The clearest account comes from Miyazu Shoten, the Takaoka company that plans, manufactures, and wholesales the kettles.

Miyazu began in 1938 as a wholesaler of cast cookware. It became Kita Shōbē's agent in 2001 and started collaborating with him on *chagama*. In 2014 it introduced a series of Shōbē wax-cast tetsubin. These dates describe the present collaboration, not the founding of an ancient Shobee workshop.

The production account is unusually specific about how the work is divided. Shōbē supervises the design and manufacture, while skilled specialists make and finish the wax-cast iron body, forged handle, and cast-copper lid. Some bodies also receive gold, silver, or copper inlay. The catalogue credits Shōbē as artist while Miyazu coordinates production and sale.

The objects show what this network can achieve. The T-12 *Clouds and Dragon* is a 1.4-litre wax-cast kettle with deep pictorial relief. Other models use phoenixes, flowers, famous landscapes, or geometric forms, sometimes with precious-metal inlay. They are not simply copies of Kyoto kettles: their dense wax modelling and multi-specialist construction are recognizably rooted in Takaoka's present metalworking economy.
