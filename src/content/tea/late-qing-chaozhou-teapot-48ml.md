<b>Base seal:</b> None<br />
<b>Base inscription:</b> Tian Qi Wan Lai Qiu (天氣晚來秋); Mengchen (孟臣)<br />
<b>Capacity:</b> 48 ml<br />
<b>Pour:</b> 20 seconds (2.4 ml/s)<br />
<b>Weight:</b> 46.06 g

This was one of the first teapots I bought when I began collecting. It is difficult to use: the pour is slow, the spout constantly clogs with tea leaves, and there is a firing crack on the handle. Still, it remains one of my favourite teapots.

Nostalgia aside, I love this pot because it is the best one I own for brewing yancha. It makes the tea taste brighter and deeper than any other clay pot or porcelain vessel I have tried. Despite the difficulties, I still use it with great pleasure because of the tea it produces. Over the years, I have somehow learned a few tricks that make it easier to use.

I bought it at an auction where it was attributed to the late Qing dynasty. Its construction, form, and marking tradition are compatible with late-Qing to Republic-period production, but these forms and inscriptions continued to be used later. Without provenance or an exact stamp match, the dating remains tentative. I like to believe it is late Qing, and the little research I have done lends some support to that attribution.

The handle bears the two-character mark 德記, read *Deji*. 德 means “virtue,” while 記 is commonly used in a traditional business or workshop name; together they identify a commercial name rather than an individual potter. A [Yu Jen auction record](https://www.yu-jen.tw/zh-tw/lot.php?act=print&id=11017) documents 德記 among the handle marks on a mixed group of comparable Mengchen-style red-clay and zisha pots. A [later online account](https://blog.sina.cn/dpool/blog/s/blog_447f741d0100kmsr.html) connects Deji with pots commissioned from the Chaozhou Yuanxing workshop, but without a matching stamp or a primary workshop record, that connection remains a lead rather than an attribution for this pot.

The base carries only 天氣晚來秋 (*Tian qi wan lai qiu*), the second half of the opening couplet in Wang Wei’s [“Autumn Evening in the Mountains”](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%B1%B1%E5%B1%85%E7%A7%8B%E6%9A%9D) (《山居秋暝》). A natural translation is “By evening, autumn is in the air.” The inscription itself does not mention mountains: 山 appears in the preceding line, 空山新雨後 (“After fresh rain in the empty mountains”), as well as in the poem’s title.

> After fresh rain in the empty mountains,  
> Autumn air drifts in at dusk.  
> Bright moonlight filters through pines,  
> Clear springs flow over stones.  
> Bamboo rustles—laundry girls return,  
> Lotus stirs—a fishing boat drifts.  
> Let spring's fragrant blooms fade;  
> A recluse may linger here.

The separate 孟臣 (*Mengchen*) invokes the celebrated potter Hui Mengchen, but poetry followed by Mengchen became a long-running convention on small gongfu teapots. It is therefore a traditional signature rather than proof of authorship.
