Among Yixing collectors, *slip cast* is often used as a polite way of saying *fake*. The phrase gets attached to anything too smooth, too cheap, too regular, or simply not charming enough. It is a wonderfully efficient insult and a terrible description.

Factory #1 really did experiment with slip casting. The experiment belongs to the factory's earliest, most ideological years, when Yixing was being asked to turn an inherited handicraft into a modern industry. It involved future masters, real zisha clay, plaster moulds, mechanical milling, leaking pots, collapsing economics, and at least one later attempt to solve the problem by changing the material until it was arguably no longer zisha at all.

That history is more interesting than the insult. It also leads to a less convenient collector's lesson: **mass-produced, mould-assisted, and slip-cast do not mean the same thing.** Factory #1 made enormous numbers of pots, but almost none of the F1 pots we normally collect were made by pouring liquid clay into a mould.

## What Slip Casting Actually Means

Slip casting is called *zhujiang* (注浆, literally “pouring slurry”) in the Chinese sources. Dry clay is milled very fine, mixed with much more water than a potter would use for hand building, and made fluid enough to pour. The slurry goes into a porous plaster mould. Plaster draws water from the clay touching its walls, gradually building a firm shell. The excess liquid is drained, the shell stiffens, and the mould is opened. Spout, handle, lid, and knob may be cast separately or made by another method and joined afterward.

For porcelain and many fine ceramic bodies, this is an extraordinarily useful process. It repeats complex forms, controls wall thickness, and reduces the amount of skilled hand-forming needed for each piece. In a factory expected to raise output and lower cost, it looks like an obvious answer.

Zisha, however, is not porcelain clay waiting to be liberated by a bucket.

## The Factory Problem Behind the Experiment

The cooperative that became Factory #1 gathered scattered workshops and senior craftspeople during the 1950s. In 1958, amid the Great Leap Forward, the Shushan cooperative, Hexin Ceramic Factory, and twenty-eight household workshops were merged into the Yixing Purple Sand Craft Factory. My [Factory #1 chronicle](/tea/yixing-factory-1-chronicle-part-1) follows that reorganisation year by year.

The new institution had two jobs that naturally pulled against each other. It was supposed to preserve and develop a celebrated regional craft, and it was supposed to behave like an efficient socialist factory. Yixing ware could earn foreign currency. That meant standard sizes, repeatable forms, lower costs, and far more pieces than the old household system could supply.

Mechanisation arrived quickly. Xu Xiutang—who entered the Shushan cooperative in 1955 and later became a China Arts and Crafts Master—uses **1957 as the dividing line**. In the preface to [*Yixing Zisha Traditional Craft*](https://www.jstaoxie.com/yxtc/index.php/Wap/tczz?id=1167), he writes that mining, crushing, refining, and forming were largely manual before then. From 1958, powered machinery entered clay preparation and plaster moulds were used to help true up bodies. The factory also tried slip casting and wheel throwing as replacements for the traditional beaten-cylinder method.

This is where three processes that collectors routinely mix together must be separated:

- **Full hand building:** clay slabs are beaten, joined into a cylinder or assembled as panels, then paddled and refined into shape.
- **Mould-assisted hand forming:** a workable clay body is built from slabs and pressed or beaten against a plaster mould to correct the form. The mould is a sizing tool; nothing is poured.
- **Slip casting:** a liquid slurry creates a shell against the inside of a porous mould.

Factory #1 adopted the second method widely. It did **not** adopt the third method widely. A plaster mould line on a spout may indicate mould use, but it does not by itself prove that the pot was slip-cast.

## 1958: The Road That Did Not Work

The firmest date in the record is 1958. Xu Xiutang's history says the factory processed zisha slurry during the Great Leap Forward but stopped because the material was unsuitable. A [later overview published by Sina](https://collection.sina.com.cn/zsbk/20120329/132861756.shtml) adds that two plaster-mould specialists from the Huishan Clay Figurine Factory in Wuxi introduced the process and that Gao Yongjun and Wang Yinxian worked on roughly two years of trials.

Tea Technique places the first tests slightly earlier—in 1955 or 1956—and says the method was abandoned around 1960. Another [account built around Wang Yinxian's recollections](https://www.zisha.com/knowledge/13889.shtml) calls it an early-1960s experiment, says it lasted more than a year, and connects its end with the factory's 1963 retrenchment. These versions cannot all be exact. The cautious reading is that **1958 was the institutional push, the work crossed the turn of the decade, and it was over by the early 1960s.**

Wang's explanation of the failure is much more valuable than a perfectly tidy date.

First, the clay needed about forty percent added water before it would flow. More water meant more drying shrinkage, more distortion, and a lower yield of usable pots. Second, some finished pots seeped enough in use to leave a wet ring on the table. Third, the sticky material accumulated on the plaster after repeated pours. Instead of producing identical bodies indefinitely, the working surface changed and the dimensions began to drift. Fourth, the moulds were consumed too quickly for the supposed economy to appear. Attempts to improve fluidity with an electrolyte made the material swell and behaved even worse.

This was not conservative craftspeople rejecting a successful machine because they feared progress. By the factory's own criteria—yield, consistency, durability, and cost—the process did not work.

There was a subtler aesthetic problem too. Tea Technique's reconstruction describes trial pots whose cast bodies are smoother, darker, and more uniform than their separately made spouts, handles, knobs, or lids. Whether every surviving example follows that pattern is doubtful, but the mechanism makes sense: milling zisha fine enough to suspend it in water removes much of the visible granular life collectors associate with the clay, while hand-built attachments preserve it.

## Why Zisha Refused to Become a Liquid

The failure begins in the structure that makes zisha desirable.

Traditional Yixing clay is not prepared as a perfectly homogeneous, glassy paste. It contains a framework of mineral grains in different sizes. The maker beats and compresses that material into slabs, creating a body that can hold a crisp unsupported form while retaining its characteristic texture after firing. The same granular, comparatively non-plastic structure that works beautifully under a paddle behaves badly when asked to remain evenly suspended in a large volume of water.

The factory could make it flow by milling it more finely, adding water, and later experimenting with deflocculants such as sodium silicate—usually called “water glass” in translations. But each solution changed something important. More water raised shrinkage and deformation. Finer milling erased texture. Additives changed the working and fired body. The mould demanded one sort of clay; the teapot tradition had evolved around another.

Xu's [history of Yixing clay preparation](https://www.dangdaiyishu.com/app/18-view-50325.shtml) shows that this resistance was not limited to teapot casting. Around 1970 the factory tried wet-grinding zisha and removing the water with a filter press. The outside of the filter cake dried while the middle remained slurry; pressure even broke aluminium filter plates. After more than a year, that experiment was also abandoned. Zisha was quite willing to enter the machine. It simply refused to come out in the form the machine expected.

## 1973: Success, with an Asterisk

The story has a second act, although the sources treat it less securely.

The Sina overview says that Xu Xiutang led another slip-casting study in 1973, with Lü Yaochen, Bao Zhongmei, Li Bifang, Zhao Hongsheng, and others. Zhao was responsible for the slurry recipe. This time, the account says, a substantial proportion of Shantou clay—or kaolin in another version—was blended with sodium silicate. The experiment produced wares whose appearance came close to handmade zisha.

That sounds like the process finally conquered the clay. Xu's own later description makes the victory much smaller.

The summary of his book [*Ten Introductory Lectures on Zisha*](https://www.megbook.hk/mall/detail.jsp?proID=2205196) describes slip-cast teapots as using a specially formulated slurry. The fired pieces are light, carry a fine slurry skin, appear dark, and ring with a sharper sound. Xu's conclusion is the important part: they belong materially to ordinary stoneware rather than true “sand ware,” because the recipe is no longer zisha clay in the traditional sense.

In other words, the reported 1973 process may have solved the manufacturing problem by changing the definition of the product. It was a technical success at making a cast teapot that looked like zisha, not proof that traditional zisha had suddenly become suitable for casting.

This also reconciles two apparently contradictory claims in the literature. Slip casting at Factory #1 was abandoned around 1960; slip casting was successfully revisited in the 1970s. Both can be true if the first refers to casting the traditional material and the second to a reformulated ceramic body.

## The Failure That Preserved a Craft

Wang Yinxian reportedly summed up the episode with a neat historical reversal: because the slip-casting road did not work, hand-made teapot production survived.

That should not be polished into a fairy tale. Factory #1 continued to mechanise mining, crushing, clay preparation, firing, and transport. Plaster moulds transformed ordinary production, making consistent shapes possible with much less training in beating the body. Xu later worried that the basic skill of *dashentong*—paddling a round body from a clay cylinder—declined because a beginner could form an approximate curve and let plaster correct it.

But the pot still had to pass through hands. A worker made slabs, assembled the body, refined it against a mould, added its parts, cut the lid, finished the surfaces, stamped it, and prepared it for firing. Factory production reorganised handwork; it did not eliminate it.

There is a lovely human arc inside this. Wang Yinxian entered the factory as a teenager in 1956 and participated in the failed attempt to make the material flow through an industrial system. She went on to become one of the great sculptural voices of modern Yixing, exploiting exactly the plastic and structural qualities that made zisha so awkward to pour.

## So What Is a “Slip-Cast F1”?

This is where history becomes an authentication problem.

Genuine experimental pieces certainly existed: Wang's complaints about brewed pots seeping onto the table describe finished, fired trials rather than a proposal on paper. Some may have left the factory, but her account of unsold wares later disposed of cheaply does not identify every one of those pots as slip-cast. Survival is plausible; a large, clearly documented population is not.

But the experiment's short duration and poor yield should make us suspicious of a market suddenly full of “rare early F1 slip-cast pots.” The phrase now covers at least four different things:

1. an actual experimental Factory #1 cast from zisha slurry around 1958–early 1960s;
2. a later cast body made from a reformulated, zisha-like stoneware slurry;
3. an ordinary mould-assisted Factory #1 pot incorrectly called slip-cast;
4. a modern industrial pot borrowing an F1 shape or seal.

There is a fifth source of confusion: **slip coating is not slip casting.** A pot can be formed conventionally and covered with a thin wash of another clay to alter its colour. Factory records mention “red outside, purple inside” and similar mixed-surface wares. The word *slip* appears in both English descriptions, but a coating tells us nothing about how the body underneath was formed.

## Clues Worth Looking For—and Clues Worth Distrusting

On a possible cast body, I would look for a cluster of observations rather than a magic tell:

- an unusually smooth, fine, uniform body with little visible granular variation;
- different colour or texture between the body and separately attached spout, handle, knob, or lid;
- a thin, even wall and an interior lacking the joining and compression traces expected from slab construction;
- casting seams or areas where seams were mechanically cleaned;
- a surface “skin” unlike the broken, granular cross-section beneath it;
- period-appropriate clay colour, firing, proportions, stamps, and workmanship everywhere else.

None of these proves slip casting by itself. Mould-pressed handles and spouts have seams. Skilled finishing can erase hand marks. Fine clay can look smooth. Wall thickness depends on the form. Sound changes with firing, wall geometry, cracks, and repairs. A “China Yixing” seal can be copied more easily than a coherent period pot can be made.

The basic method remains the same one I recommend throughout the [beginner's guide to early Yixing](/tea/beginners-guide-to-early-yixing-teapots): date the whole object. Clay, construction, form, seal, and firing must tell one story. For a supposed experimental pot, provenance matters even more because the comparison population is so small and poorly published.

I would be especially wary of confident authentication from exterior photographs alone. To discuss forming method seriously, you want clear views of the interior, underside of the lid, spout junction, handle junction, base, and any damaged edge where the body structure is visible. Even then, “consistent with” is often more honest than “proved.”

## How Collectors Should Value Them

Slip casting does not automatically make a pot worthless. A securely documented trial piece would be a fascinating industrial-history object: evidence of the moment when a new factory tried to make an old material obey a new economic system.

Nor does Factory #1 automatically make a cast pot good. The early trials were abandoned because they warped, leaked, drifted out of specification, and failed to save money. A later cast teapot may be pleasant to use, but the factory association does not restore the granular clay body or hand-forming history that the slurry recipe removed.

I would value such a pot for what it demonstrably is—not for the most romantic version of what it might be. If the attribution depends on a seller pointing at a seam and saying “rare F1 experiment,” the price should reflect that uncertainty. If the pot leaks, history will not keep the table dry.

The great irony is that Factory #1's most consequential forming experiment may be the one that failed. Slip casting promised to replace the slow hand. Instead, zisha's refusal helped determine what factory-made Yixing would become: mechanised around the edges, standardised by plaster, but still assembled and finished by people.

The machine did not save the tradition. The clay made the machine compromise.
