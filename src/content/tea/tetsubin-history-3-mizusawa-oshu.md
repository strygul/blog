## Mizusawa and the Nambu Name

Mizusawa's casting history is older than the Nambu name now attached to it.

A local tradition says that Fujiwara no Kiyohira invited casters from Ōmi in the twelfth century. The [Iwate Prefectural Museum](https://www2.pref.iwate.jp/~hp0910/tayori/101p4.pdf) treats this as a tradition rather than a proven family line. Archaeology confirms casting in the wider Hiraizumi world, but it does not show one workshop continuing without a break into modern Mizusawa.

The record becomes clearer in the late fourteenth century. The museum describes a casting site at Hada and records connected casting families. It also names Oikawa Kiemon Mitsuhiro, who began working there in 1683, as an important figure in the revival of local production.

During the Edo period, Mizusawa belonged to the Sendai domain of the Date house. Its casters became known for pots and cauldrons, along with bells, Buddhist fittings, copper goods, and cannon. The center of the local economy was practical casting.

Morioka followed another path under the Nambu domain. That story belongs in [Part 2](/tea/tetsubin-history-2-morioka). The two areas exchanged skills after the domains ended, formed a joint federation in 1959, and entered the national Nambu ironware designation in 1975.

Nambu ironware is therefore a modern shared craft name. It does not mean that Mizusawa was historically part of the Nambu domain.

## A Casting Town That Changed with Its Market

Mizusawa survived because its foundries could change what they made.

The railway reached the area in 1890. Makers sold cooking pots, cauldrons, and bath-heating parts across northern Japan and into Hokkaidō. Later, local foundries moved into vehicle parts, farm machinery, construction equipment, and machine-tool castings.

That range mattered in the twentieth century. Wartime controls restricted ordinary kettle production. After the war, aluminum, stainless steel, gas appliances, and electric kettles reduced the need for a heavy iron boiler in many homes.

Mizusawa did not depend on one object. The same knowledge of moulds, metal, heat, and finishing could serve cookware, industrial parts, or traditional tetsubin. The [Mizusawa foundry cooperative](https://oshu-nambutekki.com/about/) still represents that wide casting economy.

A Mizusawa tetsubin is made with the same basic logic described in Part 2. An outer mould carries the shape and pattern. A core creates the hollow body. Molten iron fills the gap. The casting is then cleaned, heated, colored, and fitted with a lid and handle.

The details vary by workshop and model. That is easy to see at Oigen and Oitomi.

## Oigen: Craft Inside a Modern Factory

Oigen is based in Mizusawa, Ōshū. It is a large integrated manufacturer that makes tetsubin, cookware, tableware, outdoor goods, and industrial castings.

The company uses 1852 as its founding year. Its [history](https://oigen.jp/pages/corporate-overview) explains that no exact opening record survives; the date comes from the birth year of founder Oikawa Genjūrō. Oigen first built its business around pots and cauldrons, incorporated in 1947, and later expanded its equipment and markets.

What makes Oigen special is its range of production. Some goods use repeatable company-scale moulding. More expensive kettles can use the slower *yakigata* method, in which a fired mould is broken after one pour. Current product pages may also name the craftsperson who designed or made a model.

The *Yachigusa* is a practical modern kettle with vertical ribs, a wide base, and a movable handle. The *Dragon* is very different. Oigen credits Oikawa Eiki for its hand-formed relief and describes more than eighty operations. Together they show why there is no single Oigen shape or pattern.

Oigen does publish a useful [mark guide](https://oigen.jp/blogs/contents/column06). The older house name <code>南部盛栄堂</code> appears on traditional goods. Roman-letter OIGEN marks came later, and the present logo was introduced in 2015. Some older marks remained on particular product lines, so the mark can support an identification but may not give an exact year.

## Oitomi: Family Workshop and Shop

Oitomi is also based in Mizusawa, Ōshū. It presents itself as an eight-generation family workshop, but its shop also carries kettles made by other local craftspeople.

The workshop traces its beginning to 1848, when Oikawa Rigenta founded Hōshōdō in Hada. Oitomi says he made kettles for the Date house. The name <code>及富</code>, read *Oitomi*, came from fifth-generation Oikawa Tominoshin. The company says that generation began selling abroad in the 1950s.

Oitomi describes a process that runs from mould making to final dispatch. Iron is melted in a cupola and poured into sand moulds. The casting is cleaned and ground. A tetsubin is heated inside to form a protective oxide layer, then the body, lid, and handle are colored and fitted.

What makes Oitomi different is its double role. It makes its own goods, but it also sells work by named makers and nearby workshops. A product page may credit Oitomi, Shōkadō, Seiryūdō, or another craftsperson. The seller name alone therefore does not always identify the maker.

Oitomi's own range is broad. The *Ume Maruko* uses a round body and plum-blossom surface. The *Heisei Round Arare* uses a classic field of raised dots and was designed by the sixth generation in 1989. Other models use lines, dragonflies, gourds, smooth skin, and modern colors.

These patterns can help find a catalogue match, but none belongs only to Oitomi. Read the full product page and check whether it names Oitomi or another maker.

Oitomi does not publish a dependable guide to kettle seals. Its website documents the workshop name, but that is not enough to date or identify every cast mark. When no exact mark comparison exists, use the model, construction, box, receipt, and workshop confirmation.
