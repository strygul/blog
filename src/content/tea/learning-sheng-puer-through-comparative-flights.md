Even though I have been drinking tea for as long as I can remember, I came to conscious tea drinking much later in life—and, somewhat unexpectedly, through wine. Living in a country where tea drinkers are a small minority, I found it difficult to meet like-minded companions. Without a community or a clear path to follow, I had to pursue most of my tea education on my own, as many people in the West probably do: relying on whatever online resources I could find and chaotically buying different teas with no purpose beyond exposing myself to as many of them as possible.

This kind of broad exploration is probably an important—and inevitable—step in the journey of anyone who wants to understand tea as something more than a hot drink consumed after dinner with a slice of cake. It introduces you to the extraordinary diversity of tea and helps you discover your own preferences. But it can take you only so far. At some point, you begin to wonder whether you are still learning anything simply by trying yet another new tea.

Coming to tea through wine made me increasingly aware—not that tea lacked educational traditions, but that I could not find a clear path through them. Wine education offered me a visible framework for comparing regions, vintages, producers, grape varieties, and methods of production. In tea, the knowledge was certainly there, but from the perspective of an independent learner in the West, it often felt scattered across traditions, specialist books, blogs, vendors, and online communities. What I was missing was not information itself, but a practical sequence that could turn that information into purposeful tasting.

I began to find the beginnings of such an approach through blogs and social media, where people recommended paired, horizontal, and vertical tastings. I tried them and learned a great deal. They showed me how much more one can discover by comparing teas with a particular question in mind. Still, as a beginner, I often lacked the knowledge and context needed to choose meaningful comparisons—or to make every tea session and every tea purchase as useful for learning as possible.

Over the years, I accumulated more knowledge, discovered many excellent tea resources, and retained the same thirst for learning and desire to deepen my understanding of tea. Gradually, the idea emerged to create the kind of practical guide I wish I had when I was starting out: a guide that would bring structure to tea exploration without taking away the pleasure of discovery. It is this guide that I would now like to share with anyone who might find it useful, inspiring, or insightful.

Because sheng puer is one of my all-time favourites, it felt like the natural place to begin. The program introduces twelve basic flights and twelve advanced flights: suggested comparative tastings that explore some of the variables that make one sheng different from another. The accompanying research includes suitable teas, vendors, and estimated prices.

## Why comparative flights

Broad tasting builds familiarity, but it can also lose direction. A comparative flight begins with a question and puts two or more teas beside one another, directing attention to age, storage, terroir, recipe, producer, construction, or market framing.

These are practical orientations, not controlled experiments. Year, batch, material, processing, compression, storage, and sampling history may remain confounded, so a flight is a way to ask better questions rather than to assign one cause to every difference. The named teas are suggestions: when stock changes, a substitute should preserve the lesson before the label.

## The twelve core flights

1. **Youth and transformation** asks how a recognizable 7542 recipe can change from current production through middle and late-semi-aged examples, while keeping batch and storage differences in view.
2. **Storage changes the clock** introduces the way storage labels can frame transformation, without treating unmatched teas as proof that climate alone caused the result.
3. **Factory and boutique** asks what a large-factory reference and a more disclosed small-brand construction reveal when their age, format, and price scale are kept reasonably close.
4. **Yiwu and Bulang** introduces broad eastern-Xishuangbanna and Bulang/Menghai orientations while keeping origin and tree-age language attached to vendor claims.
5. **One tea, two storages** asks what can be learned from the same named production offered with two explicitly described storage histories.
6. **7542 and 8582** introduces recipe numbers as useful points of comparison without turning leaf-grade language into a quality hierarchy.
7. **Menghai and Xiaguan** asks how factory identity, recipe, compression, age, and stated storage interact instead of presenting the pair as a pure house-style comparison.
8. **Xishuangbanna and Lincang** introduces a broad regional question using origins that the product pages actually state.
9. **Boutique lineage through time** asks how a named boutique lineage looks across a young and a late-semi-aged tea while acknowledging different recipes, origins, and unknown storage.
10. **Humid-labelled and traditional Hong Kong storage** introduces a storage-school orientation while separating humid wording from evidence of traditional Hong Kong treatment and airing.
11. **Blend and single-area origin** asks what disclosed blend construction and a vendor-stated single named area can teach when the producer label, harvest, portion, and market tier are close.
12. **Three-age 7542 sequence** revisits 7542 in young, 10–20-year, and over-20-year bands, making storage, batch, and vendor changes part of the lesson.

## Beyond the core: twelve advanced flights

These optional electives are not a second required course; they extend the core questions when curiosity, availability, and budget allow.

13. **Yiwu within Yiwu** asks how several vendor-labelled Yiwu or Mengla subregions compare while holding one producer and current-harvest frame as close as possible.
14. **The Lao Man’e bitterness spectrum** examines explicitly bitter- and sweet-varietal descriptions from Lao Man’e while keeping the limits of vendor framing visible.
15. **Mengku-side and Bangdong-side Lincang** asks how two disclosed Lincang-side orientations compare without inferring provenance beyond the product pages.
16. **Spring and autumn from one named origin** explores seasonal selection from the same named origin while treating tree-age and craft details as vendor claims.
17. **Age-oriented and aroma-preserving construction** contrasts disclosed construction orientations, not a controlled processing test.
18. **Natural-garden and ancient-garden claims** compares explicitly labelled garden histories without treating those labels as independently verified tree age.
19. **The Dayi 7532, 7542, and 8582 suite** asks what a same-year Dayi recipe suite discloses and leaves unknown about recipe numbers.
20. **Xiaguan recipe and compression forms** extends the Xiaguan question by keeping recipe, compression, batch, year, and storage claims distinct.
21. **Three boutique disclosure philosophies** compares lineage-led, blend-led, and origin-led presentations without making a quality or vendor ranking.
22. **From a purchasable pair to a storage-city triangle** contrasts a matched two-storage pair with a broader multi-production storage orientation.
23. **Traditional Hong Kong intensity and airing evidence** asks which facts distinguish traditional Hong Kong treatment, intensity, and post-cellar airing from a general storage label.
24. **Reputation, adjacency, and normalized value** explores how a famous-name tea and a disclosed nearby origin can shape price and expectation without implying equivalence.

## How I chose the teas and vendors

[TeaDB's Pu'erh hub](https://teadb.org/puerh/), [The Five Types of Raw Pu'erh You Should Try](https://teadb.org/five-types-raw-puerh/), and the [2025 Non Mainland Pu'erh Vendor Guide](https://teadb.org/non-mainland-puerh-vendor-guide/) were my compass for categories, hypotheses, and vendor discovery—not a source of current prices or guaranteed tasting outcomes. Current product pages supplied portion size, availability, price, and vendor-stated product and storage facts.

The core relies on Yunnan Sourcing for closely matched contemporary comparisons; King Tea Mall for sample-scale Dayi and Xiaguan references; Liquid Proust for unusual storage and Xizi Hao lineage samples; and Yee On for explicitly traditional Hong Kong storage examples. Farmer Leaf, Tea Encounter, white2tea, Teas We Like, and Bana Tea Company enter advanced comparisons or alternatives.

I also investigated Tea Urchin, Essence of Tea, Crimson Lotus Tea, The Jade Leaf, Quiche Teas/Taishunhe, Pu-erh.sk, Puerh.uk, Hou De, Teapals, Yangqing Hao USA, and other TeaDB leads. Their absence from the final offers is not a quality judgment: an otherwise appealing lead could lack a matched current comparison, available samples or stock, complete product or storage detail, or a precisely verifiable offer.

This shortlist is not an overall vendor ranking, and its prices are not necessarily the lowest prices worldwide. Educational fit, sample access, documentation, reuse across flights, and fewer separate orders all mattered alongside price. Where a page names an origin, tree age, batch, or storage history, that language remains a vendor claim unless otherwise noted.
