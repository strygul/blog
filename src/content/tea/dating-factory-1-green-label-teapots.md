<div class="info-box">

This article grew out of a discussion with my fellow collector <a href="https://www.instagram.com/ever.ythingtea/">@ever.ythingtea</a> about a Green Label teapot I recently added to the blog, <a href="/tea/factory-1-late-70s-shuiping-75ml/">my 75 ml Factory 1 Shuiping</a>.

When I first published the pot, I attributed it to the late 1970s. He thought it more likely to belong to the early 1980s, and rightly challenged how confident I was about my attribution.

In collector circles there are several ideas about how to tell an early Green Label Shuiping from a late one. Some have a basis in fact. Others are conjecture, and a few have been repeated often enough to pass for fact. That discussion made me curious enough to dig through the English and Chinese sources and see which of those ideas hold up. With the pot in hand, how confidently could I really tell the difference?

</div>

## What Does Green Label Mean?

Collectors call the late 1970s and early 1980s the Green Label period after the oval green *Made in China* sticker used on teapots sold for export. The dates vary depending on the source. [TeaPotWay][labels] dates the sticker to **1977–1982**, and [Tea Technique][english] gives **1977–1983**. In my [earlier post on Factory 1 labels](/tea/early-teapots-factory-1-green-white-and-laser-labels/#green-label-egg-shaped-label), I used a broader range, **1977–1986**, that includes some of the unlabelled years that followed.

A green sticker on its own tells me little about who made a pot or when. [Reproduction stickers][english] can make a later pot look like an older one. TeaPotWay also reports that distributors sometimes received [loose stickers to apply themselves][labels]. Even an original sticker gives only a rough date.

This was a period of growing exports and higher production at Factory 1, with [orders from Hong Kong merchants][labels] helping drive demand. Some collectors describe the clay and workmanship on everyday Green Label pots as less refined than on earlier pieces. It was also a time of experimentation with moulds and clay. TeaPotWay describes [changes to the moulds for some Shuiping bases][base-moulds], and the [provincial ceramics history][gazetteer] records a change to red-clay bodies from the earlier red-coated bodies in 1984.

I briefly touched on these differences in my [comparison of Shuipings from the 1960s through the 1990s](/tea/f1-shuiping-shape-evolution/). Here I want to go a little deeper into the Green Label years. What can the body, lid, workmanship, clay and seals tell us about whether a Shuiping comes from the earlier or later part of the period?

## Moulds

The Green Label years fell at a time when Factory 1 was introducing revised moulds for Shuiping bodies and lids. The changes in shape and finish can make some pots from this period easier to recognise. The changeover took several years, with older and newer tools in use at the same time. That overlap makes it hard to pin down when an individual pot was made. I would treat the details left by the moulds as hints and weigh them alongside other pointers.

### Body moulds

In its [write-up of a red-clay six-cup Shuiping][base-moulds], TeaPotWay says a groove where the floor meets the wall was common on 1970s pots. The collector puts a change to the base mould after the turn of the 1980s and says potters added a step to fill the groove. Here are the interiors of two six-cup Shuipings of about 120 ml from the same collector.

On a Green Label Shuiping, a smoother join can hint at the later way of finishing the base that TeaPotWay describes. TeaPotWay dates the grooved example only to the 1970s and gives no year when potters stopped leaving the groove open. As I noted in my [post on flat and grooved bases](/tea/early-teapots-flat-base-vs-grooved-base/), Factory 1 used a different technique in the 1960s. The flat inner base met the wall directly, so there was no groove to fill. A [collector writing about standard Shuipings][sina] says several sets of moulds were in use at the same time and that worn moulds had to be replaced. I would weigh this detail alongside the other clues when deciding between the late 1970s and early 1980s.

### Lid moulds

The potter [Xu Hantang recalled][xu] using iron tools to make lids by 1959 because they were quicker than plaster. Both methods were in use during the Green Label years. TeaPotWay dates the iron-pressed lids below to the late 1970s and early 1980s, respectively.

The tools affected the shape of the lid, right down to the skirt, the wall that fits inside the pot’s opening. In [TeaPotWay’s workshop photos][iron], the potter puts a sheet of clay between two iron pieces and strikes the top piece with a hammer. A short skirt makes the tool easier to pull out. Twisting it free can leave a swirl in the soft clay.

With plaster, potters could shape the top first and [attach the skirt afterwards][phone]. This made it easier to give the lid a taller skirt. Potters could also make short skirts in plaster or taller ones with a different iron tool. TeaPotWay’s [mid-1980s four-cup Shuiping][four] shows what one plaster-moulded lid from around that time looks like.

When did iron pressing fall out of use? A [collector who phoned Dingshan][phone] reported that the factory was using only plaster by **1982–83**. A [factory potter quoted by TeaPotWay][iron] remembered using iron tools until about **1983–85**. Both accounts put the change near the end of the green sticker years or shortly after. The collector linked it to the factory adding production lines faster than it could supply them with iron tools.

There is some basis for the idea that earlier lids look puffier and later ones flatter. [TeaPotWay describes iron-pressed lids][iron] as rounded on top, with a sharper turn where the top meets the edge. Lin, the collector who phoned Dingshan, describes a [gentler curve on plaster-moulded lids][phone]. Another [collector writing about standard Shuipings][sina] describes a thin lip, the outer edge of the lid, on 1960s and 1970s pots and a thicker one on 1980s pots. These are patterns collectors have noticed. With both types of mould in use around 1980, I would give the curve and the thickness of the edge little weight on their own when choosing between the late 1970s and early 1980s.

## Craftsmanship

It is tempting to read a neatly joined handle or a crisply finished spout as a sign of an earlier pot. The difficulty is that several things besides the year could affect those details. In [TeaPotWay’s account of workshop practice][master], several potters could be working on the same model at once. A plaster mould gradually lost its sharp edges with use, while the potter still had to attach and finish parts by hand. Even a fresh mould left room for differences between workers.

I have found no dated run of otherwise comparable Shuipings showing that a rougher joint or heavier wall specifically marks the early 1980s.

## Clay

Clay can help date a Shuiping when we can identify the material and have a written account of when Factory 1 used it. I found several clays associated with the early 1980s, with dates that also reach back into the late 1970s. The clearest year in the records is **1984**, when the factory changed how it made one series of red Shuipings.

### Niangao clay

*Niangao tu* (年糕土), or “rice-cake clay”, is a [Taiwanese collectors’ name][nian] for a fine, dense clay that can fire towards red or purple. [Lin associates niangao Shuipings with the early 1980s][lin-clay], and TeaPotWay dates the six-cup example below to that time. A [2016 copy of TeaPotWay’s write-up][nian-feed] gives about **1981–85**, but the [current version][nian] gives **the late 1970s to about 1985**. Those are two versions of the same account. TeaPotWay also suggests the nickname may cover two similar materials. If the identification is sound, niangao points towards the early 1980s, with the late 1970s still possible.

### Jiazi clay

*Jiazi ni* (甲子泥), also called black-star clay, is a purple clay blend known for its dark specks. [TeaPotWay’s guide to Factory 1 clays][factory-clays] puts its main use at about **1976–85**, with trials already taking place in the late 1970s. The collector links the nickname to **1984**, a *jiazi* year in the Chinese calendar. The stated range covers both sides of 1980. Potter [Xu Xiutang also describes small dark iron spots][xu-clay] on fired zisha more generally, so a few black specks are too little to identify the blend.

### Red clay and red slip

[TeaPotWay’s clay guide][factory-clays] says the red clay known as *xiao hongni* (小紅泥) was used in the 1960s and 1970s and became scarce towards the end of the 1970s. One response was *nei zi wai hong* (內紫外紅), “purple inside, red outside”. The factory coated a purple-clay body in red slip, a liquid mixture of clay and water. On [TeaPotWay’s 200 ml Shuiping][slip], some of the slip ran through the spout opening onto the darker clay inside. The collector puts this series at about **1978–84**, spanning both the late 1970s and early 1980s.

The [Jiangsu provincial ceramics history][gazetteer] records that red-clay Shuipings replaced the red-coated ones in **1984**. That year dates the change for this series. [TeaPotWay also describes][factory-clays] a shift to red clay from Huanglongshan in the 1980s as the earlier sources ran short. [Lin’s account][lin-clay-history] lists a Huanglongshan red clay among the earlier materials too. Even the mine name leaves room for more than one date. I found no factory record that ties a recognisable Shuiping clay exclusively to the early 1980s.

### Was the earlier clay better?

A [collector account of early Shuipings][clay-quality] argues that demand rose in the 1980s and the factory gave the mined clay less time to weather before processing it. That is a specific claim about how the clay was prepared. The account gives no batch records or tests comparing late-1970s and early-1980s Shuipings. [Xu Xiutang recalls][xu-clay] that the factory processed several grades of clay as early as 1958. I found evidence that supplies and recipes changed. I found no comparison that establishes that late-1970s clay was better across the board.

## Seals

Some seals can help narrow down when a Green Label Shuiping was made. Sometimes the clue is as small as an extra character in an export seal. On other pots, it is the pairing of the base stamp with a number inside the lid. Collector accounts link both of these clues to Shuipings made in the early 1980s.

### Six characters on the base and a number in the lid

[Yu Qiren (余啟任)][numbered] dates Shuipings with 荊溪惠孟臣製 (*made by Hui Mengchen of Jingxi*) on the base and a number stamped with metal type inside the lid to **1980–86**. He gives that range after making a telephone inquiry. [TeaPotWay’s 200 ml Shuiping][slip] is an example of the pairing. It has a small **0** in its lid, and its owner dates it to the early 1980s.

How much can either mark tell us on its own? [TeaPotWay puts the use of lid numbers to count a workshop’s output][slip] as early as the late 1970s. The same collector describes the stamp above, known as the “disordered six characters” (亂六字章), as typical of the early 1980s. [Another collector dates a Shuiping with that style of base stamp and a name inside the lid][six-character-name] more broadly to the late 1970s or early 1980s. Yu’s **1980–86** range applies to the two marks together.

### The 23-character Qing Yin export seal

Some [*Qing Yin* Shuipings](/tea/early-teapots-the-qing-yin-series/), the ones inscribed “Please drink Chinese oolong tea”, carry the name of the Xiamen export company on the base. On the 23-character version, look for the extra **總** in 進出口**總**公司, part of the company name.

[The collector Taoren (陶仁) recalls handling a whole batch][qing-yin-batch] of early-1980s *qingshuini* Qing Yin Shuipings. He found both 22- and 23-character stamps in that batch and says he had never seen the 23-character version on a 1970s red-clay Qing Yin. [TeaPotWay also lists both versions][qing-yin-four] for the early-1980s purple-clay four-cup run. That gives the extra character some weight when dating a Shuiping from this series. The 22-character version turns up in accounts of both decades.

### The four-character China Yixing seal

Even the same style of 中國宜興 (*China, Yixing*) stamp can turn up on both sides of 1980. TeaPotWay identifies the **7:3** style on two six-cup Shuipings of about 120 ml. The collector dates [the red-clay one][base-moulds] to the 1970s and [the niangao one][nian-seal] to the early 1980s.

[Taiwan’s Yingge Ceramics Museum][museum] records that some Yixing teapots had their base marks ground off before they were brought into Taiwan, when imports from mainland China were restricted. A rough patch where a stamp once sat can be a clue to that history. A smooth, blank base may simply have been left unstamped.

## Where That Leaves My Shuiping

For [my 75 ml Shuiping](/tea/factory-1-late-70s-shuiping-75ml/), I still lean slightly towards the **late 1970s**. It is the low dome and short skirt seen against the shoulder that give me that impression. The small flaws in the finish and the width of the foot ring carry little weight. I recorded the clay as *qingshuini* (清水泥), and its 7:3 中國宜興 base seal and unmarked lid give me no reason to narrow the date. The [potter’s recollection of working with iron lid tools into the 1980s][iron] is what made me take the later date seriously.
