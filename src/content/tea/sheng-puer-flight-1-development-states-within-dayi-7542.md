## What This Flight Asks

How do three selected Dayi 7542 productions—with different stated years, batches, and storage histories—differ under
one brewing protocol?

This is a useful first flight because it offers three chronological points while retaining the same producer and recipe
label. The comparison should be capable of producing observable contrasts without requiring the taster to identify any
particular flavor. If the differences are not clear or do not repeat, **inconclusive** is a valid result.

This is not a controlled age experiment. Year changes together with batch, source material, manufacture, compression,
storage, sampling history, and custody. The purpose is to map the three teas as they exist now, not to claim that age
alone caused the differences.

Buying all three samples gives enough tea for at least two 5 g comparison sessions. Two sessions matter: a contrast

## Why This Set

All three teas come from one vendor, carry the same producer and recipe label, and are available in practical sample
sizes. That removes some obvious noise and makes a three-point comparison feasible without buying full cakes.

It does not make the teas identical apart from age. A recipe number is not a fixed bill of materials, and the set does
not control production batch, leaf material, processing, compression, or storage. Think of the flight as a comparison
of three **development states**, with chronology and storage history intertwined.

## Set Up the Flight

You will need three identical neutral porcelain vessels, three matching cups, a scale, a timer, one kettle, and enough
of the same water for the whole session.

1. Weigh **5 g of each tea** into identical vessels with a practical brewing volume of **100 ml**.
2. When possible, take similarly situated pieces from each sample rather than comparing a compressed edge with loose
   fragments from the center.
3. Have another person assign neutral codes such as A, B, and C. If you are tasting alone, conceal the names as far as
   practical and put the key out of sight before brewing.
4. Preheat every vessel in the same way. Use the same water at **100 °C** for all three teas.
5. Give each tea a five-second rinse, then brew for **10, 10, 15, 20, 30, and 45 seconds**.
6. Keep water volume, pour speed, and drinking order consistent throughout the session. Randomize cup position when the
   codes are assigned, and use new positions for the repeat session.

Pouring three vessels introduces timing differences. Choose a fixed pouring sequence, work briskly, and use the same
sequence in every round. If one infusion is obviously mistimed, mark it rather than forcing it into the conclusion.

## What to Observe

Do not begin by guessing which tea is oldest. For every code, follow the same dimensions across the infusions:

- liquor color and clarity;
- dry-leaf, warmed-leaf, and cup aroma families;
- bitterness onset, intensity, and duration;
- where astringency is felt and how long it persists;
- texture and apparent body;
- sweetness and returning sweetness;
- aftertaste;
- the direction and pace of change from one infusion to the next.

Use descriptive notes before preference scores. “Bitter at the sides of the tongue and gone before the next sip” is
more useful than “harsh”; “dense texture through infusion four” is more useful than “better.” You are looking for a
repeatable pattern, not the vocabulary you think a particular vintage ought to produce.

Before the reveal, write one short summary for each code and rank the three teas by preference. Only then uncover the
identities and add the storefront information beside your notes. Do not rewrite the blind observations after the
reveal.

## Repeat Before Drawing a Conclusion

Repeat the complete comparison on a different day with new codes and cup positions. Keep the leaf-to-water ratio,
water, vessels, temperature, rinse, infusion schedule, and pouring sequence unchanged.

Compare the two sessions dimension by dimension. A useful result does not require identical wording, but the principal
contrast should recur—for example, the same tea repeatedly showing earlier bitterness, longer astringency, or greater
textural persistence. If the main contrast disappears, reverses, or remains unclear, report the flight as
**inconclusive**.

## What the Result Can Support

The allowed conclusion is modest: **this is how these three selected Dayi 7542 productions differed across their stated
development histories under this protocol**.

The flight cannot establish an age-only effect, a universal 7542 aging trajectory, the authenticity of any label, or a
specific storage cause. Even a clear and repeatable sequence belongs to these samples. It is evidence for a comparison,
not a law about every 7542 production.

That limitation does not make the flight weak. It makes the exercise honest: the differences may be tasted, described,
and retested without pretending that tasting alone can identify why they exist.

## If One Sample Is Unavailable

Keep Dayi 7542 as the common producer and recipe label, and retain at least three meaningfully separated production
years. Prefer samples from one seller with documented batches. Disclose storage gaps before relaxing seller or batch
matching, and restart the blind comparison rather than treating a replacement as interchangeable with the original
set.

The 2007 sample returns in Flight 6, and all three teas can also support the product-documentation exercise in Lab B.
That reuse is one reason this flight belongs at the beginning of the program.
