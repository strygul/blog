## Yamagata and Its Casting Tradition

Yamagata casting is traditionally traced to the late Heian period. The story says Minamoto no Yoriyoshi brought casters north after a military campaign, and one settled in the area. The exact family line is hard to prove, but metalworking was well established by the time Yamagata became a castle town.

In the early seventeenth century, the Mogami rulers gathered casters in Dōmachi, the city's metalworking district. They made temple bells, Buddhist objects, pots, tools, and goods for the castle town. Kettles were one part of a much wider industry.

The regional craft is now called Yamagata Imono, or Yamagata cast metal. It received national traditional-craft recognition in 1975. The [official craft profile](https://kougeihin.jp/en/craft/0702/) highlights thin walls, accurate shapes, and clean surfaces as regional strengths.

That does not mean every Yamagata kettle looks alike. The three workshops below use the same regional skills in very different ways.

## How Yamagata Makes a Kettle

Yamagata makers work with several kinds of mould. A turned mould can build a round body around a central guide. A pressed pattern can add arare dots or other decoration. More complex shapes may use wax or a one-use mould that is broken after casting.

The basic steps are familiar. An outer mould gives the kettle its shape and surface. A core creates the hollow interior. Molten iron fills the space between them. The body is then cleaned, heated, colored, and fitted with a lid and handle.

What stands out is control. Yamagata kettles are often lighter than they appear. Spouts are carefully finished, walls can be thin, and decoration can range from a plain cast skin to detailed relief and metal inlay.

## Seikodo: Detailed Surfaces and Lost Wax

Seikodo was based in Dōmachi, Yamagata's old casting district. Its [prefectural profile](https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/02_Cast-metal%EF%BC%88final-version%EF%BC%89_2024.pdf) traces the family workshop to 1735, when its first head, Kiroku, made tetsubin.

Later generations pushed the surface work further. The sixth head, Tokutaro, became known for lost-wax casting. The eighth head also studied Japanese painting and brought that eye for composition to tea kettles and chagama. In its later catalogues, the workshop offered both tea-ceremony kettles and a wide range of tetsubin.

That long line ended recently. Seikodo stopped operating in June 2025, and the Yamagata District Court began bankruptcy proceedings in March 2026, according to a [Yamagata Broadcasting report](https://news.livedoor.com/article/detail/30887132/).

Seikodo is special because it does not depend on one look. Some models use lost wax for lions, dragons, flowers, and deep relief. Others are much quieter. The *Fuku-Arare* uses hand-pressed dots across a low round body, while the tall *Konatsume* leaves most of the cast surface plain.

Those details can help with a catalogue match, but they are not unique to Seikodo. Lost-wax relief and arare patterns also appear elsewhere. The safest comparison uses the full shape, lid, spout, decoration, and workshop record together.

The workshop name is written <code>清光堂</code>, read *Seikodo*. The sources used here do not publish a reliable guide to the workshop's kettle seals, so the name alone should not be used to identify an unknown mark.

## Kikuchi Hojudo: Old Foundry, Modern Shapes

Kikuchi Hojudo is another Dōmachi workshop. Its [official history](https://www.wazuqu.jp/roots/) begins with a written record from 1604, when the first Kiheiji worked as a caster under the Mogami house.

The workshop did not begin as a tetsubin specialist. It made shrine objects and practical household goods, then changed with the market. During the Second World War, its traditional production stopped and the factory was directed to make grenades. It returned to household metalwork after the war. That long history belongs to the foundry as a whole, not to every kettle design now in its catalogue.

Kikuchi Hojudo stands out for bringing old casting skills into simple modern forms. The round *Hikime* has fine horizontal lines that follow the body. The square *Yoho* uses broad flat sides and a rectangular handle. Both are made to boil water, but neither looks like the pictorial Seikodo kettles above.

A [Government of Japan workshop profile](https://www.gov-online.go.jp/eng/publicity/book/hlj/html/201108/201108_14.html) also describes attention to clean pouring and handles inspired by Japanese swords. These are useful design clues, not proof of origin. Round bodies, square bodies, and linear surfaces can all be copied.

The workshop name is <code>菊地保寿堂</code>, read *Kikuchi Hojudo*. Its public pages do not provide a dependable kettle-seal guide. For an exact identification, compare the whole object with a named model rather than guessing from an isolated stamp.

## Chobundo: A Postwar Kettle Workshop

Chobundo is based in Imono-machi, another part of Yamagata City. It was established in 1952, which makes it much younger than Seikodo or Kikuchi Hojudo.

Its history is directly tied to iron kettles. First-generation Choroku Hasegawa trained as a caster and became a factory manager around 1945. His son Fumio later opened the workshop and formed the name Chobundo from one character in each of their given names. The [2024 Yamagata catalogue](https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/02_Cast-metal%EF%BC%88final-version%EF%BC%89_2024.pdf) names Mitsuaki Hasegawa as the third-generation maker.

Chobundo is known for light bodies, handmade spouts, and restrained shapes. The tall *Natsume* was designed by the founder and takes its form from the jujube fruit. The low *Komagata* is covered with arare dots and finished with a pine-cone knob and a small bamboo-leaf detail.

The catalogue says the mould for the *Komagata* is used once, then broken. It also describes a baked lacquer finish that becomes glossier with use. Those details apply to the named model; they should not be stretched into a rule for every old Yamagata kettle.

The workshop name is <code>長文堂</code>, read *Chobundo*. Like Kikuchi Hojudo, it does not publish a clear guide to kettle marks. The tall *Natsume* shape is strongly associated with the workshop, but a shape by itself is still not a seal.
