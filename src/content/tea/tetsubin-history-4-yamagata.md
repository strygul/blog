<div class="info-box">Yamagata's casting history is much older than any one workshop—and older than the tetsubin itself. The chronology here is built from official Yamagata craft records, government and association histories, workshop records, museum material, and HOJO's Seikodo visit. Foundry dates do not automatically become tetsubin dates; I keep the two apart.</div>

### Nine centuries of casting, not nine centuries of tetsubin

Yamagata is often introduced with a round number: nine hundred years of casting. It is a useful way to locate a regional tradition, but a poor shortcut to the age of a particular kettle. The [Traditional Crafts Aoyama Square account](https://kougeihin.jp/en/craft/0702/) gives the familiar origin tradition: in the middle Heian period, foundry workers accompanying Minamoto no Yoriyoshi found suitable river sand and local clay, and some settled in the present Yamagata area. That is a regional origin account, not a dated catalogue of tetsubin.

The same official account gives a second, later anchor. About four centuries ago, seventeen foundry workers were gathered into Dōmachi. The town became a concentrated casting district, making religious implements and daily goods. It was not a moment when every later Yamagata product appeared at once.

The official craft record lists tea kettles, iron teapots, bronze vases, iron pots, ornaments, Buddhist statues, temple bells, and other cast work. A kettle workshop can grow out of this shared ecology without making the old regional date its own foundation date. The tetsubin itself remains an eighteenth-century form in the broader national chronology described in Part 1.

Yamagata casting was nationally designated a Traditional Craft on 17 February 1975. Its protected specification makes the regional vocabulary unusually legible: sand moulds, true clay at the metal-contact surface, turning or filling methods (including wax casting), firing or drying, natural lacquer and iron-oxide coloring for ironware, and anti-tarnish treatment for cooking vessels. Those are category rules, not a claim that every maker uses every route on every object. “Lost wax,” for example, is a permitted and documented route, not a magic label to attach to a richly decorated kettle merely because its surface looks intricate.

### The Yamagata method

The visible result of this system is often called *usuniku-birei*: thin wall, beautiful casting surface. The phrase names a regional aspiration, not a measurable guarantee for every old or new kettle. In the association’s turn-mould account, a wood model is rotated while sand is compacted into upper and lower moulds. A *nakago* core creates the interior. Handle lugs are embedded; arare or other patterns are drawn or pressed into the mould; the core is dried and fired; iron is poured; the breakable mould and core are removed; the casting is fired, filed, polished, colored, and fitted with its lid and handle.

Wall thickness is the controlled space between core and outer mould, not a sales ranking. Arare is made in the mould before pouring; a spout, rim, or glossy surface cannot name a workshop by itself. The association’s lacquer-and-oxide sequence is regional context, while producer claims below remain model-specific.

### Seikodo

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open the evidence:</strong> the <a href="https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/02_Cast-metal%EF%BC%88final-version%EF%BC%89_2024.pdf">Yamagata export portal profile and catalogue</a>, <a href="http://www.seiko-do.com/">Seikodo’s official site</a>, the <a href="https://kougeihin.jp/en/craft/0702/">Yamagata association record</a>, and the supplied <a href="https://hojotea.com/item_e/seikodo.htm">HOJO visit</a>. HOJO is used only as attributed workshop observation; dates and product routes are checked against the export record.</div>

**Chronology.** Seikodo has the clearest start date in this group: the prefectural export portal records “Starting: 1735,” while also distinguishing the 1966 establishment of the incorporated company. The profile says the first family head, Kiroku, began with cast-iron products and represents tetsubin as part of that workshop account. This is strong first-party, government-hosted chronology, but it does not turn 1735 into a date for every Seikodo kettle or prove an uninterrupted product range over nearly three centuries.

The same profile describes a later concentration on *chagama*. Its account says postwar demand for tea-ceremony kettles led to the present company’s specialization in them, while the ninth and tenth family heads also produced a large tetsubin range in response to demand from the 1990s onward. That is more interesting than a generic “oldest” claim: one house has navigated religious and tea implements, a modern company structure, and changing kettle markets. It also keeps the small spouted tetsubin separate from the broader-mouthed, ladled chagama.

**Process.** This is the one profile where lost wax is concrete evidence rather than a stylistic guess. The export catalogue labels three named iron kettles “wax type” and says they are produced by lost-wax casting; their dragon, lion-and-peony, and dragon-spout reliefs are described there. It also documents hand-pressed arare on another hiramaru kettle. These are model-level statements. They do not license the inference that every Seikodo kettle, every historical object, or every elaborate dragon surface was made by lost wax.

The sixth head is described in the export profile as skilled in lost-wax work and as exhibiting tetsubin and chagama at international expositions; the account attributes award success to those exhibitions. A separate catalogue field lists Takumi Sato for current models. Read this as a workshop-published lineage and current product credit, not as a complete historical authorship ledger. HOJO’s visit may help readers imagine a working studio, but it cannot authenticate a detached handle, lid, or object on the secondary market.

**Visual language.** Seikodo’s documented range is unusually expansive: hand-pressed arare, smooth *natsume* and octagonal bodies, combed bands, iron-sand kettles, dragon and landscape relief, copper lids, silver knobs, and gold or silver inlay. The sources make the fittings part of the design rather than an afterthought: a vermilion copper lid and silver pine-needle knob belong to named current objects; several iron-sand models have matching iron-sand lids. These are strong catalogue descriptions, not a visual fingerprint that can date an orphan kettle.

Yamagata casters’ inspection of advanced places including Kyoto documents regional contact, not a Seikodo teacher, technical transfer, or model genealogy. “Kyoto influence” is therefore not an attribution shortcut here.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Seikodo is best described through the documented 1735 start, later chagama specialization, and an unusually specific current catalogue.

**Representative objects.** The 2024 prefectural catalogue securely attributes named current objects to Seikodo: wax-type *Ryū-guchi Ryūjimon*, Fuku-arare, and Hakkakkei-odare, each with materials and dimensions. That is a comparison set, not a historical identification chart. [Open the catalogue](https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/02_Cast-metal%EF%BC%88final-version%EF%BC%89_2024.pdf) for the dated source record.

### Identifying Seikodo

No authoritative Seikodo tetsubin mark guide, dated seal sequence, or two complete kettle-and-mark pairs were located in the accessible record. The company name is written `清光堂`, read *Seikōdō* (romanized here as Seikodo); the literal meaning is not used as an attribution claim. The absence of a published guide is not evidence that kettles were unmarked. It is an evidence ceiling.

### Kikuchi Hojudo

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open the evidence:</strong> the Government of Japan’s <a href="https://www.gov-online.go.jp/eng/publicity/book/hlj/html/201108/201108_14.html">2011 Kikuchi Hojudo feature</a>, the <a href="https://www.wazuqu.jp/shop/testubin_m/index.html">official WAZUQU tetsubin page</a>, the workshop’s <a href="https://www.wazuqu.jp/roots/">roots page</a>, and the <a href="https://kougeihin.jp/en/craft/0702/">Yamagata casting association record</a>. The government article’s credited photographs are not reused: page visibility and a photo credit are not a reuse license.</div>

**Chronology.** Kikuchi Hojudo’s decisive date is not “the 1600s.” The government feature says its oldest written company record dates to 1604, when Kiheiji Kikuchi gathered seventeen people to make cast-iron products. It also records the family account that the history may be older. That second claim must remain attributed: the first surviving written record is not proof of a pre-1604 workshop, and the record does not show that the original group began by making tetsubin.

The feature gives a useful product sequence. The group became known for iron candle holders and shrine items, then moved into practical things including teapots and sake containers. That trajectory belongs to Yamagata’s broad casting economy and protects against an easy anachronism: a 1604 foundry record is not a 1604 tetsubin record. Noriyasu Kikuchi was identified in 2011 as the fifteenth family president. This is a dated report, not a statement about who leads the workshop in 2026.

The wartime record is equally important. The government feature says the firm was ordered to make grenades during the Second World War and returned to its core products afterward. Such interruption makes “unchanged for four centuries” the wrong story. Its longer continuity is a working capacity to cast, teach, redesign, and restart—not an unchanged catalogue.

**Process.** The feature says skills in smelting and casting pass from senior workers to juniors through an extended apprenticeship lasting up to ten years. It reports that about half of the iron used at the time was recycled. Both are company-reported in a government feature; neither identifies a mould route for a particular kettle. The regional association documents sand moulds, true clay, turning or filling, drying or firing, and lacquer-and-oxide coloration; it is sound regional context, but not a license to state that every Kikuchi Hojudo piece follows each step.

There is no accessible evidence here for calling Kikuchi Hojudo’s kettles dry-mould or lost-wax work. Aoyama Square allows dried or fired moulds and wax casting within the protected regional craft; that does not choose a technique for this producer. The shortest honest wording is: the workshop works inside the Yamagata casting tradition, while the accessible sources do not publish the model-level mould route needed to classify an individual kettle.

**Visual language.** The government report describes traditional iron teapots alongside brightly colored, rounded-corner square teapots developed for contemporary and export markets. It attributes sword-inspired handles and attention to non-dripping spouts to the company’s own explanation. These statements identify design intentions in a 2011 feature, not a reliable authentication key. A square body, color, or a handle resembling a sword cannot prove Kikuchi Hojudo without a complete object and provenance.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Kikuchi Hojudo’s documented 1604 foundry record, practical product evolution, apprenticeship, and postwar return matter more than an undocumented exact mark or timeless silhouette.

**Representative objects.** The first-party [WAZUQU tetsubin page](https://www.wazuqu.jp/shop/testubin_m/index.html), accessed in 2026, documents *Fuku* (600 cc) and *Yoho* (900 cc) as water-boiling tetsubin, separate from its teapot range; it supplies dimensions and weights. The official [roots page](https://www.wazuqu.jp/roots/) also names a 1926 exposition-award tetsubin. These secure current/historical contexts do not supply an old-object mark or mould route.

### Chobundo

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open the evidence:</strong> <a href="https://chobundo.jp/">Chobundo’s official site</a>, its <a href="https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/2024/06/11/993/">Yamagata export portal profile</a>, the detailed <a href="https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/02_Cast-metal%EF%BC%88final-version%EF%BC%89_2024.pdf">prefectural cast-metal catalogue</a>, and the <a href="https://kougeihin.jp/en/craft/0702/">regional association process</a>. Workshop photographs remain external because no reusable license was located.</div>

**Chronology.** Chobundo was established in 1952. The portal and catalogue agree on that date, and the catalogue calls iron kettles its main product. Its workshop story gives the date a human scale: first-generation Choroku Hasegawa trained and became a factory manager around 1945, then began realizing a long-held wish to make iron kettles. After he died, second-generation Fumio Hasegawa established the studio name Chobundo, built from a character from each man’s name; the 2024 export material identifies Mitsuaki Hasegawa as the third-generation maker.

This is a young workshop inside an old regional craft. It is not less Yamagata for beginning in 1952, and it should not borrow the region’s nine centuries as a foundation date. Unlike the other two profiles, its history is directly and consistently kettle-centered in the published workshop account. That gives the date a precise object relationship without pretending the family made every regional casting form from the start.

**Process.** Chobundo gives unusually clear model-level evidence. Its Natsume kettle is described as thin-wall casting with a handmade spout; its Kodai Hiramaru and Komagata descriptions say their casting mould is made for each piece and not reused; the Komagata and other kettles state a Japanese-lacquer coloring finish. The portal also names *urushi yakitsuke chakushoku*—baked lacquer coloring—as a final finish. These descriptions support a breakable, one-use mould context for those named models. They do not prove the precise inner-core construction, timing of every process, or treatment of every historical Chobundo kettle.

**Visual language.** Chobundo’s current evidence starts with the tall Natsume: a 1.5-litre, 1.4-kilogram iron kettle whose upright jujube-like silhouette is presented as the founder’s design. The smaller version keeps the long vertical body. Kodai Hiramaru is flatter and plainer; Komagata carries arare, a pine-cone knob, and a subtle bamboo-leaf detail; Zakuro uses pomegranate and bee relief; Fuji-zakura and Kiku bring floral imagery and, on Kiku, a vermilion copper lid. The source gives weights, dimensions, and material for these current listings, providing useful comparison rather than historical proof.

The documented intent is utility joined to surface control: lightness, clean pour, stable profile, and a finish meant to deepen with use—not a promise about an unknown antique.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Chobundo’s solid identification story is 1952, kettle specialization, and named present models with one-use mould and lacquer-finish descriptions. Its profile shows how a postwar workshop can continue Yamagata’s material language without pretending to be a medieval survivor.

**Representative objects.** The 2024 prefectural [Chobundo catalogue pages](https://craft.yamagata-export.jp/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/02_Cast-metal%EF%BC%88final-version%EF%BC%89_2024.pdf) securely identify the current Natsume, Kodai Hiramaru, and Komagata kettles with dimensions, weights, named maker, and process notes. Those current records are useful comparison anchors; they are not a mark guide for older objects.

### Three workshops, three continuities

The workshops share regional standards, not one history. Seikodo’s 1735 record includes later chagama specialization and model-specific lost wax. Kikuchi Hojudo’s oldest written foundry record is 1604—not a tetsubin date. Chobundo’s 1952 kettle-focused account documents thin walls, one-use moulds, spouts, and lacquer.

The warning is deliberately repeated because the market rewards shortcuts. Lids, handles, boxes, certificates, and receipts can be replacements or later pairings; a signed lid authenticates only the lid unless it is independently connected to the body. Marks can be copied, and workshops also made unmarked work. Arare, a thin wall, a copper lid, a dragon, a familiar spout, or “Yamagata style” can support a comparison but cannot prove a maker, region, date, or original assembly. The safe conclusion for an unproven object may be: “Japanese iron kettle, Yamagata-type comparison possible; maker and date uncertain.”
