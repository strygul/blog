This article continues the investigation begun in [Part 1](/tea/tetsubin-history-1-birth-of-the-iron-kettle). I started with HOJO's visits to [Suzuki Morihisa](https://hojotea.com/item_e/suzuki.htm) and [Kunzan](https://hojotea.com/item_e/kunzan.htm), then checked the story against workshop histories, craft associations, government records, and published research. The sources do not always agree. Where they differ, I keep the uncertainty instead of forcing a cleaner story.

## Morioka and the Problem of an Old Name

Part 1 left us with a useful warning: **an old casting tradition does not make every object from that tradition equally old**.

Morioka shows exactly why that warning matters.

The city is one of the two main centers now gathered under the name Nambu ironware. Its casting history reaches back about four hundred years, to the time when the Nambu domain recruited skilled metalworkers to make bells, Buddhist objects, household vessels, and tea kettles.

Even the beginning of that story refuses to fit into one tidy date. The [Suzuki workshop](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/nanbu-ironware-and-the-suzuki-family) and [Morioka City](https://www.city.morioka.iwate.jp/_res/projects/default_project/_page_/001/024/890/2syou.pdf) say the first Suzuki caster entered domain service in 1625. A [2019 Iwate University paper](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/sfj/70/5/70_255/_pdf/-char/ja) gives 1641, when Nambu Shigenao invited Suzuki Ietsuna from Kōshū.

Perhaps the two dates describe different stages of the family's arrival and service. None of the sources explains that, so both dates have to remain.

The tetsubin came later. Morioka tradition credits the third Koizumi Nizaemon with turning the older tea kama into a smaller kettle with a handle and spout around 1750. As we saw in Part 1, that is a strong regional tradition—not proof of the first tetsubin made anywhere in Japan.

This distinction changes the story. Morioka did not spend four hundred years making the same black kettle. It carried old casting knowledge through new tea habits, new markets, war, industrialization, and modern design. The four workshops in this article show four very different ways of doing that.

## How Morioka Makes a Kettle

A finished tetsubin looks solid and permanent. Its birth is anything but.

The [Nambu ironware association](https://www.ginga.or.jp/nanbu/making.html) describes a process built around sand, clay, fire, and destruction. First, a rotating template shapes the outer mould. The maker presses arare dots, rough skin, lines, or other decoration into that mould. The pattern is already waiting before the iron arrives.

A separate core creates the hollow interior. The small space between core and mould becomes the wall of the kettle. The spout is moulded separately and joined to the body mould. Then molten iron is poured into the remaining space.

After the iron cools, the mould is opened or broken. What comes out is recognizably a kettle, but not yet a finished one. Casting channels and seams must be removed. The body is checked, the interior is heated to form a more stable oxide layer, and the outside receives lacquer and traditional coloring. Only then are the lid and forged handle fitted.

More than eighty operations may stand between the first line of the design and the finished kettle. That does not mean every workshop divides the work in the same way. One may pass the kettle between specialists. Another may let one craftsperson carry it through most of the process. Iwachu can make traditional tetsubin by one route and enamelled teapots by another.

That variety is the key to Morioka. **Nambu ironware describes a regional tradition, not one factory and not one method.**

## Suzuki Morihisa: The Domain Lineage

If Morioka has a courtly line, it is Suzuki Morihisa.

The workshop traces its service to the Nambu domain to 1625, though the Iwate University paper gives the 1641 date noted above. The name *Morihisa* came later. According to the [workshop history](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/nanbu-ironware-and-the-suzuki-family), it became the hereditary name with the twelfth head after the Meiji Restoration. The old shop burned in 1884 and moved the following year.

The present head is [Suzuki Morihisa Shigeo, the sixteenth](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/introduction-of-the-master). He entered production in 2008, joined management in 2014, and succeeded to the name in 2023. That recent succession matters because older articles still describe the previous head as current.

It also corrects a more persistent claim. HOJO's visit calls the thirteenth head a Living National Treasure. The workshop itself uses narrower language: in 1974 he was designated an “intangible cultural asset for record-keeping.” A search of the Agency for Cultural Affairs holder database did not establish the Living National Treasure title. The database warns that its records are incomplete, so this is not proof that no honor existed. It does mean the larger title should not be repeated as settled fact.

Suzuki's long lineage can make the workshop sound like one master working alone. Its own account describes a team. The hereditary head designs and supervises, while craftspeople carry the kettle through the breakable-mould process and its finishing stages.

The current [product catalogue](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/collections/all) is much livelier than the word “traditional” might suggest. There are calm arare surfaces and fine ribs, but also broad grooves, flower patterns, and playful geometries. The nearly spherical *Hinomaru* is linked to the thirteenth head; the interlaced *Temari* to the fifteenth. Tradition here is not one frozen shape. It is a workshop continuing to design.

The published mark evidence is useful but limited. Horie's paper shows `盛久`, read *Morihisa*, in variants associated with the thirteenth and fourteenth heads. These body marks were pressed into the mould with wooden seals and usually sit low near the spout. The figure does not show complete kettles, so a similar mark on an unknown object is a promising clue—not a complete attribution.

## Kunzan: A Branch Becomes Its Own Workshop

Kunzan has no need to borrow Suzuki's age. Its own story is interesting precisely because it begins much later.

The [workshop history](https://nanbu93.jp/pages/company) starts with Asada Kaoru, born in 1904. He trained under Suzuki Kyūtarō at Suzuki Shuzendō, then established his own workshop in 1937. Sasaki Kazuo began learning the craft in 1972 and became the third Kunzan in 1976. In 1994 he was certified as a nationally recognized traditional craftsperson. The workshop is now based at Morioka Handi-Works Square.

This is how a craft tradition grows: not only through an unbroken family line, but also through apprenticeship and departure. Kunzan learned inside the Suzuki world, then formed a separate workshop with its own name and work.

Kunzan says its patterns are made by hand, its moulds are fired and broken, and its casting remains in-house. That gives us a good picture of present production. It does not tell us that one person performs every operation, or that every old Kunzan kettle followed exactly the same route.

Its [current catalogue](https://nanbu93.jp/collections/all) resists easy stereotypes. Fine and coarse arare appear beside rough surfaces, horizontal lines, tortoiseshell geometry, and plant designs. Round bodies sit beside flatter forms. There is no single “Kunzan look” that can authenticate an anonymous kettle across a room.

The mark evidence is thinner. Retailers report `薫山`, read *Kunzan*, and sometimes `南部盛岡`, or *Nambu Morioka*. I found no workshop guide showing how those marks were made, where every version appears, or when each was used. An original box, invoice, catalogue match, or confirmation from the workshop would therefore carry far more weight than the surface pattern alone.

## Iwachu: Craft at Industrial Scale

Iwachu is the workshop that breaks the romantic picture most usefully.

Its own English-language pages give three dates for its beginning. The [company overview](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/about-us) says “since 1901” in one place and June 1908 in its corporate information. The [history page](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/history) says Iwashimizu Sueyoshi opened the business in 1902 after four years of training.

Those dates may refer to the start of work, the opening of a shop, and later corporate organization. The pages do not explain the difference. “Early twentieth century” is the honest answer.

The business became Iwashimizu Foundry in 1950 and expanded its integrated factory in the early 1970s. Today Iwachu makes traditional water-boiling tetsubin, enamelled iron teapots, cookware, and other cast goods. Its scale is not an embarrassing exception to the craft story. It is part of the story: Nambu ironware survived partly because producers found new objects and new customers.

Iwachu explains the difference through two production routes. Its [tetsubin process](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/manufacturing-process) uses the traditional fired mould, hand-pressed patterns, a core and separate spout, an oxide-treated interior, and exterior lacquer. Its [teapot and kitchenware process](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/teapot-kitchenware-process) includes automated moulding and vitreous enamel inside the teapots.

This is more than a workshop detail. It is a practical safety test. An enamelled Iwachu teapot is made for steeping tea and should not be placed over direct flame. A traditional tetsubin is made to boil water. If you are unsure which object you have, look inside before looking at the logo.

Iwachu publishes four current `岩鋳` or `IWACHU` devices on its authenticity page. They can support a present-day Iwachu identification, but the page does not date each design or show where every mark sits on a complete kettle. A logo tells you less than sellers often suggest.

## Kamasada: One Maker, One Kettle

Kamasada brings us back from the factory to the individual workbench—but not to an untouched past.

Its roots lie in the Miya casting family. A [Morioka City history](https://www.city.morioka.iwate.jp/_res/projects/default_project/_page_/001/024/890/2syou.pdf) records Miya Gonjirō as a caster in the early Meiji period. His third son, Miya Sadakichi, established a kettle shop in Konyachō near the end of Meiji. The name Kamasada came with the second head, Miya Shōtarō.

Those are three connected events, not one foundation date. An older family tradition, the opening of a shop, and the adoption of a house name should not be compressed into a single convenient year.

The workshop's history also contains a real break. Wartime controls forced it to dismantle in 1945. It rebuilt in 1954 using timbers from the earlier structure. That detail is more moving than a claim of perfect continuity: the workshop survived because somebody chose to begin again.

Modern Kamasada work is closely associated with Miya Nobuho and with spare, controlled forms. Rough yuzu-like surfaces, low round bodies, broad iron handles, and quiet silhouettes allow the casting itself to carry the design. The [Cabinet Office](https://www8.cao.go.jp/shokun/hatsurei/r06aki/meibo_hosho/hosho-03iwate.pdf) records Miya receiving the Yellow Ribbon Medal in 2024 for excellence in hand moulding. That is the documented honor; it is not a Living National Treasure designation.

Two institutional accounts help explain the work. A [Japan Economic Foundation profile](https://www.jef.or.jp/journal/pdf/172th_Japanese_Craft.pdf) counts about forty hand operations. A [Toraya exhibition account](https://assets.toraya-group.co.jp/__/_files/toraya/pdf/press_release/20160818_252.pdf), prepared with Kamasada, counts about eighty. They divide the work differently, but agree on the important point: one craftsperson carries a kettle through the sequence instead of handing each stage to a separate specialist.

Retail pages report `南部 釜定` below the spout on some work and `KAMASADA` with a year beneath the lid on a modern line. No workshop or museum guide establishes a complete historical sequence for those marks. The famous rough surface is no shortcut either; other workshops use rough casting skins. With Kamasada, as with the other makers, the whole kettle and its history matter more than one attractive clue.

## What the Four Workshops Teach Us

I began this part of the investigation looking for clean differences between four makers. I found four different ways of carrying a craft forward.

Suzuki Morihisa preserves a hereditary domain lineage through a collaborative workshop. Kunzan shows how apprenticeship can branch into a new house. Iwachu joins traditional kettles to industrial production and an international market. Kamasada concentrates many operations in the hands of one maker and turns restraint into a modern design language.

All four belong to Morioka. None can stand in for Morioka by itself.

They also make identification less dramatic and more reliable. Start with the entire object, not the signature. Is it an un-enamelled water boiler or an enamelled teapot? Record the body, spout, lid, handle, interior, seams, and marks before separating anything. Compare the mark with a dated source. Then look for the evidence that belongs to that exact kettle: its box, receipt, catalogue number, workshop correspondence, or museum history.

Arare dots are regional vocabulary, not a maker's fingerprint. Rough skin is not uniquely Kamasada. A signed lid may have moved from another body. Names can be copied, and genuine workshops also made unmarked pieces.

Sometimes the most accurate identification is simply **“Morioka-type tetsubin, date and maker uncertain.”** That may feel less satisfying than a famous name. It is still more interesting than a false certainty, because it leaves the real question open.

Morioka gave the tetsubin one of its strongest origin stories and some of its best-known workshops. But the modern Nambu name also includes Mizusawa and Ōshū, where the casting history begins from a different place.
