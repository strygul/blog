### The domain behind the iron

Morioka gives Nambu its courtly history. Edo-period rulers recruited casters for bells, Buddhist implements, vessels, and tea kettles. The “four hundred years” belongs to that casting lineage, not to the later spouted tetsubin.

Even Suzuki family service has two dates. The [workshop](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/nanbu-ironware-and-the-suzuki-family) and [Morioka City](https://www.city.morioka.iwate.jp/_res/projects/default_project/_page_/001/024/890/2syou.pdf) give 1625; Horie’s [Iwate University paper](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/sfj/70/5/70_255/_pdf/-char/ja) gives 1641, when Nanbu Shigenao invited Suzuki Ietsuna. None explains the discrepancy or supplies a contemporaneous record that resolves it. The dates may describe different stages, but that remains inference; both stay visible.

Morioka’s tetsubin history is later. Tradition credits the third Koizumi Nizaemon with adapting the tea kama into a smaller handled, spouted kettle around 1750. Nineteenth-century foundries widened the market and developed patterns, relief, colored finishes, and named marks.

“Nambu ironware” now covers both Morioka and Mizusawa/Ōshū, centers with different histories joined institutionally and commercially in the modern period. This article stays in Morioka; the next returns to Mizusawa.

### The Morioka method

The [Nambu association](https://www.ginga.or.jp/nanbu/making.html) describes a rotating template shaping a sand-and-clay outer mould. Arare dots or rough *hada* are made in the mould. A core forms the hollow interior; the separately moulded spout is assembled before pouring, cooling, and release.

After gates and seams are removed, heat treatment stabilizes the interior oxide layer; lacquer and iron solution color the exterior. Handle and lid are fitted separately. [Aoyama Square](https://kougeihin.jp/craft/0701/) describes over eighty operations and specialist handle makers: “handmade” need not mean one person.

Morioka City records early-twentieth-century research, wartime stoppage, and postwar rebuilding. The 1975 national designation formalized shared techniques, not one workshop model; small studios and integrated companies both belong. Ask which process made the object.

Horie says Morioka kettles were initially unmarked, then acquired freely written family names in mid-Meiji. Matsuhashi Sōdai encouraged workshop or art names pressed into the mould with wooden seals. Documented marks sit low near the spout and are easiest to see with the kettle inverted. Current practice is not uniform.

### Suzuki Morihisa

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open the evidence:</strong> Suzuki’s <a href="https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/nanbu-ironware-and-the-suzuki-family">first-party family history</a>, <a href="https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/introduction-of-the-master">current-head profile</a>, and <a href="https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/collections/all">live product index</a>; Horie’s <a href="https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/sfj/70/5/70_255/_pdf/-char/ja">authoritative mark reference</a>. Linked images remain external and are neither republished nor authenticated here.</div>

**Chronology.** Suzuki Morihisa is the oldest lineage in this comparison, though its opening date remains the 1625/1641 conflict described above. The [workshop’s history](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/nanbu-ironware-and-the-suzuki-family) says the first Suzuki caster was recruited from Kōshū in 1625 to serve the domain. It also explains that *Morihisa* became the hereditary workshop name with the twelfth head after the Meiji Restoration. The old shop burned in 1884 and moved the next year. The current head is [Suzuki Morihisa Shigeo, the sixteenth](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/pages/introduction-of-the-master), who succeeded in 2023 after entering production in 2008 and company management in 2014.

The succession corrects two problems in HOJO’s otherwise valuable workshop account. Its page predates the latest transition. It also gives the thirteenth head a national-treasure honorific. The official workshop uses narrower wording: in 1974 he was designated an “intangible cultural asset for record-keeping.” On 31 July 2026, an Agency for Cultural Affairs holder-database search returned no entry for `鈴木盛久`, `鈴木繁吉`, or `鈴木重吉`; the [database scope note](https://kunishitei.bunka.go.jp/bsys/about) warns that records are still being added. This limited search is not proof of absence, but the available evidence does not establish the **Living National Treasure** title repeated by retailers.

**Process.** Suzuki describes its production as collaborative rather than the work of a solitary named master. The hereditary head designs and supervises within a workshop that preserves the breakable-mould tradition. HOJO’s visit is useful on this limited point because it observed multiple craftspeople working through organized stages. That observation agrees with the regional production sequence, but it cannot establish how every Suzuki object—especially an older one—was made.

The records do not give model-level role divisions, production records for isolated kettles, or proof that replacement handles and lids are original. The fired mould is broken after release; finishing and fittings follow casting.

**Visual language.** Current Suzuki kettles range from disciplined geometric skins to playful silhouettes. Arare, fine vertical ribs, broad combed grooves, and restrained rough casting recur across the catalogue. The overall bodies are often compact and clearly profiled, with fixed arched iron handles and close-fitting lids. Yet these are current catalogue tendencies, not a diagnostic grammar handed down unchanged from 1625.

**Representative objects.** The current [Hinomaru](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/products/%E6%97%A5%E3%81%AE%E4%B8%B8%E5%BD%A2%E9%89%84%E7%93%B6-%E5%B0%8F) is nearly spherical, with fine horizontal ridges and a round handle; the design is credited to the thirteenth head. The 1.3-litre [Temari](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/products/%E4%BA%88%E7%B4%84%E5%95%86%E5%93%81-%E6%89%8B%E6%AF%AC%E9%89%84%E7%93%B6-%E5%A4%A7), associated with the fifteenth head, turns interlacing ball geometry into the main ornament. A [flower-crest *santama*](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/products/%E5%B0%8F%E8%8A%B1%E5%B0%BD%E7%B4%8B%E7%AE%97%E7%8E%89%E5%BD%A2%E9%89%84%E7%93%B6) uses flowers pressed individually into the mould, while the [peony *natsume*](https://suzukimorihisa.com/en/products/%E7%89%A1%E4%B8%B9%E7%B4%8B%E6%A3%97%E5%BD%A2%E9%89%84%E7%93%B6) joins floral relief to a gardenia-shaped knob. These current examples should not back-attribute historical kettles.

**Maker identification.** The strongest published comparison is Horie’s academic figure showing `盛久` marks associated with the thirteenth and fourteenth heads. It documents the characters and generational variation, and his regional account explains wooden seals pressed into the mould and the usual low position near the spout. The reproduced mark crops do not include their complete kettles. Retail pages may pair a current seller-attributed kettle with a similar mark, but that is not independent verification of the object or a historical sequence.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Suzuki stands apart through a documented hereditary identity carried into a present collaborative workshop, not through a single texture or presumed performance effect. The most defensible attribution combines mark form, its location in the casting, the complete kettle’s construction, and a dated chain of ownership. Without those connections, “in the Suzuki style” is more accurate than “by Suzuki Morihisa.”

### Kunzan

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open first-party evidence:</strong> Kunzan’s <a href="https://nanbu93.jp/pages/company">company history</a>, <a href="https://nanbu93.jp/pages/genuine">process statement</a>, and <a href="https://nanbu93.jp/collections/all">product gallery</a>. No authoritative mark guide was located; retailer mark pages are discovery leads only. Linked images remain external and unauthenticated here.</div>

**Chronology.** Kunzan is a twentieth-century workshop, not a second ancient dynasty. Its [official company history](https://nanbu93.jp/pages/company) begins with Asada Kaoru, born in 1904, who trained under Suzuki Kyūtarō at Suzuki Shuzendō and established his own workshop in 1937. Sasaki Kazuo, born in 1945, began studying the craft in 1972 and succeeded as the third Kunzan in 1976. The workshop records his 1994 certification as a nationally recognized traditional craftsperson. Today it operates at Morioka Handi-Works Square; the Nambu association lists Sasaki Kazuo as its chair and Kunzan as an active member workshop.

That chronology matters because it shows how lineages branch. Kunzan learned within the Suzuki orbit but built a separate house name, production practice, and body of work. The link is apprenticeship, not permission to collapse the two brands or read Suzuki authorship into a Kunzan kettle.

The regional craft is older than this separately documented 1937 workshop.

**Process.** Kunzan’s [authenticity statement](https://nanbu93.jp/pages/genuine) emphasizes manual patterning, a mould that is fired and broken, and casting retained within the workshop. It also gives characteristic body and base thicknesses for its own kettles. Those figures are manufacturer specifications, not a universal Nambu rule. HOJO’s visit adds an observation of family and team labor and discussion of mould choices. It is useful evidence about the visited workshop at that time, but not a substitute for production records for every catalogue line.

“In-house” supports workshop control; it does not mean one person performs every specialized operation. Present statements cannot prove an older kettle’s route, mark method and sequence, or that its fittings are original.

**Visual language.** Kunzan’s current range is broad. Fine and coarse arare sit beside rough *hada*, horizontal thread-like bands, tortoiseshell geometry, and pictorial motifs. Fixed iron handles and iron lids keep many forms visually dark and unified, while some knobs and special designs introduce other materials. The catalogue suggests a workshop comfortable with both conservative round bodies and decorative variation. None of those features is exclusive to Kunzan.

**Representative objects.** The [current workshop catalogue](https://nanbu93.jp/collections/all) makes the range concrete. A two-litre Nambu form in iron-sand casting carries arare across a 178-millimetre body. A 1.3-litre *shinjū* form uses fine horizontal *itome* lines and a larger mouth. The 1.4-litre *tetsubachi* and flatter 1.6-litre *hiramaru* return to arare with different proportions. Tortoiseshell and plant motifs elsewhere in the catalogue confirm that “Kunzan” does not mean one texture. An anonymous kettle with the same motif still needs independent evidence.

**Maker identification.** Current retail galleries report `薫山` (*Kunzan*) and, in some descriptions, `南部盛岡` (*Nambu Morioka*). The workshop’s own history and catalogue pages do not provide a technical mark guide, dated seal sequence, or complete-object/mark pairs. Retail photographs can suggest a comparison target, but they do not independently establish who made the pictured kettle, how the lettering was formed, or how long a given device was used. The regional near-spout convention cannot fill those gaps.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Kunzan’s strongest identifying story is a dated apprenticeship branch followed by an active workshop that states its present production approach. Its range makes surface-only attribution especially weak. A credible assignment needs the `薫山` name in complete-object context, agreement between construction and catalogue record, and preferably a box, invoice, or workshop confirmation tied to that same object.

### Iwachu

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open first-party evidence:</strong> Iwachu’s <a href="https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/about-us">company and current-mark page</a>, <a href="https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/history">history</a>, <a href="https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/manufacturing-process">kettle process</a>, and <a href="https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/tetsubin">live kettle guide</a>. Its mark close-ups remain external and are not whole-object authentication.</div>

**Chronology.** Iwachu is the industrial-scale participant in this group, founded in the early twentieth century by Iwashimizu Sueyoshi after training as a caster. Its own English pages disagree over the exact milestone: the [company overview](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/about-us) says “since 1901” in its prose and June 1908 in corporate information, while the [history page](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/history) says Sueyoshi opened the business in 1902 after four years of training. The discrepancy may separate commencement, opening, and corporate organization, but the pages do not say so. “Early twentieth century” is therefore firmer than selecting one foundation date.

The postwar company became Iwashimizu Foundry in 1950 and expanded its integrated production. Iwachu now makes hand-finished tetsubin alongside enamelled teapots, cookware, and other cast goods. The Nambu association lists representative director Iwashimizu Yayoi in its current leadership. That scale and range are not departures from history to hide; they are central to understanding the firm.

The company history places its integrated-factory expansion in the early 1970s.

**Process.** Iwachu usefully publishes two different process accounts. Its [tetsubin sequence](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/manufacturing-process) shows the traditional *yakigata* route: a profile template shapes the mould, patterns are pressed by hand, the mould is fired, core and spout parts are assembled, iron is poured, and the finished interior and exterior receive their respective treatments. Its [teapot and kitchenware process](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/pages/teapot-kitchenware-process) describes automated moulding and an enamelled interior for teapots. A colorful Iwachu teapot is not therefore evidence that its water-boiling kettles are enamelled too.

Official care guidance reinforces that split: enamelled teapots are steeping vessels and should not go over direct flame, while the kettle route specifies an oxide-treated interior and exterior lacquer. Inspect the interior before assigning a production route.

**Visual language.** Because Iwachu serves several markets, its visual field is unusually wide. Tetsubin include conservative arare globes, ribbed and rough-skinned forms, flatter bodies, and pictorial surfaces. The broader teapot range introduces saturated colors and smaller brewing forms. Visual identification must begin by separating an un-enamelled water boiler from an enamelled steeping pot, then move to the specific catalogue model.

**Representative objects.** The current [23-gata Nambu arare](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/products/11001) is a 1.9-litre, 2.2-kilogram water boiler with a kiln-fired interior and lacquered dark-brown exterior. A [0.9-litre arare model](https://iwachu.co.jp/en/products/11720) is documented for induction heating. The enamelled teapot line uses a separate repeatable workflow. These product families prevent “Iwachu” from being treated as one object type or one method.

**Maker identification.** Iwachu publishes a current authenticity page with four `岩鋳`/Iwachu seal designs. That is valuable first-party evidence for current brand marks. It is also incomplete identification evidence: the page calls them seals or stamps but does not specify their manufacturing stage, their position on a kettle, date ranges for each design, or full-object pairings. A Latin `IWACHU` variant is among the present marks, alongside Japanese forms.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Iwachu’s distinguishing evidence is organizational and process-specific: an integrated firm that openly separates traditional kettle production from automated, enamelled goods. The name on an object must be interpreted with product type, lining, construction, and catalogue context. A current mark can support an Iwachu attribution; it cannot by itself date a kettle or tell which production route made it.

### Kamasada

<div class="info-box"><strong>Open authoritative workshop evidence:</strong> no first-party Kamasada gallery was located. Use the <a href="https://www.ginga.or.jp/nanbu/cooperative.html">Nambu association</a>, <a href="https://www.city.morioka.iwate.jp/_res/projects/default_project/_page_/001/024/890/2syou.pdf">Morioka City history</a>, <a href="https://assets.toraya-group.co.jp/__/_files/toraya/pdf/press_release/20160818_252.pdf">Kamasada-assisted process account</a>, and <a href="https://www8.cao.go.jp/shokun/hatsurei/r06aki/meibo_hosho/hosho-03iwate.pdf">Cabinet Office honor record</a>. Retail images remain external and unauthenticated here.</div>

**Chronology.** Kamasada’s roots lie in the Miya casting family. The [Morioka City report](https://www.city.morioka.iwate.jp/_res/projects/default_project/_page_/001/024/890/2syou.pdf) records Miya Gonjirō operating in the early Meiji period and says his third son, Miya Sadakichi, established a kettle shop in Konyachō near the end of Meiji. The source supports that period but not a precise foundation year. The name Kamasada came with the second head, Miya Shōtarō.

That sequence separates the older Miya family activity, Sadakichi’s shop, and the later Kamasada name. Treating them as one undifferentiated founding event would turn family antecedents into a false brand date. On an object, `釜定` identifies the house name; it does not by itself determine which Miya generation made the casting.

The workshop once sent popular kettles and tea-kama to Hokkaidō and Kantō. Wartime controls forced dismantling in 1945. In 1954 it rebuilt with timbers from the earlier structure—an unusually concrete example of recovery rather than uninterrupted operation. Kamasada remains active in Konyachō, and the Nambu association lists Miya Nobuho among its current directors. Japan’s [Cabinet Office records](https://www8.cao.go.jp/shokun/hatsurei/r06aki/meibo_hosho/hosho-03iwate.pdf) Miya, the current representative, receiving the Yellow Ribbon Medal in 2024 for excellence in hand moulding. That is the documented honor; it is not a Living National Treasure designation.

**Process.** Two institutional accounts add workshop-specific detail. A [2010 Japan Economic Foundation profile](https://www.jef.or.jp/journal/pdf/172th_Japanese_Craft.pdf) describes about forty hand operations, a fired sand-and-clay mould, interior *yakinuki* treatment at roughly 900°C, and exterior lacquer. A [2016 Toraya exhibition](https://assets.toraya-group.co.jp/__/_files/toraya/pdf/press_release/20160818_252.pdf) made with Kamasada describes the chain more finely: about eighty operations, not divided among specialists, with one craftsperson carrying a piece through the sequence. These accounts differ in how they count an “operation,” but agree on integrated handwork. Neither proves that every product line or every historical Kamasada kettle followed exactly the same route.

The mark-forming method and whether present fittings are original also remain unknown.

**Visual language.** Kamasada’s current attributed work often favors restrained bodies in which silhouette and casting skin carry the design. Yuzu-like roughness, low round forms, spare lids, and broad iron handles recur. Other objects use arare or small grouped motifs. The workshop is also known through modern cast-iron design beyond kettles, which helps explain the controlled geometry without making “modern” a dating test.

**Representative objects.** The retailer-documented current [Yuzu kettle](https://www.cotogoto.jp/view/item/000000001370?category_page_id=kamasada) provides one attributed form reference with an irregular citrus-like skin; it is not an independent attribution record. Institutional accounts show that Kamasada’s modern design identity cannot be reduced to one kettle texture.

**Maker identification.** Retail pages report a below-spout `南部 釜定` (*Nambu Kamasada*) device and a separate modern line with Latin `KAMASADA` plus a year beneath the lid. I found no workshop mark guide, museum record, or technical study that establishes the forming method, introduction dates, or a historical seal sequence. The photographs are therefore leads for comparison, not verified mark/context pairs.

**Evidence-based distinction.** Kamasada is most defensibly distinguished by the combination of its Miya-family/Konyachō history, restrained attributed designs, and product-specific marking. Rough yuzu skin alone proves nothing; it appears elsewhere. A `釜定` body mark in a complete current context or the dated under-lid `KAMASADA` system is stronger, especially when supported by the original box or purchase record.

Begin identification with a complete kettle. Establish whether it is a boiler or enamelled teapot. Record body, spout, lid, handle, interior, seams, and marks before separating components. Compare mark form and location with a dated source, then seek a box, receipt, catalogue number, workshop correspondence, or museum provenance tied to the object.

No single feature carries attribution. Arare is regional vocabulary; rough skin is not uniquely Kamasada. A signed lid may have moved, names can be copied, and workshops made unmarked objects. **Mark alone is not authentication.** Sometimes the sound conclusion is “Morioka-type tetsubin, date uncertain,” with the missing evidence named.
