<h2>Why This Set</h2>

The close commercial format makes the comparison practical and limits some obvious sources of noise. It also places a
Lincang example beside a Xishuangbanna example without a large prestige or price mismatch.

The pair is intentionally an orientation. If the teas differ clearly, the observation still belongs to these two
samples. If they overlap or the contrast does not repeat, that is equally valid information.

<h2>Flight-Specific Controls</h2>

Keep the product pages, map, and expected regional descriptions out of view. Consult them only after the first blind
notes and preference ranking are complete.

<h2>What to Observe</h2>

Record each code on the same dimensions:

- dry-leaf, warmed-leaf, liquor, and cup aroma;
- bitterness onset, intensity, location, and duration;
- sweetness and returning sweetness;
- astringency location and persistence;
- texture and apparent body;
- finish and cooling behavior;
- perceived development and infusion-to-infusion change.

Avoid turning one striking note into a regional marker. The principal contrast should be a pattern across several
dimensions or infusions, not a single expected word.

<h2>What We Can Learn From It</h2>

This tasting shows us how these Xishuangbanna- and Lincang-labelled teas differed when we brewed them in the same way.

Two teas are not enough to tell us what either whole region tastes like. Harvest, leaf material, processing, compression, and storage may explain some of what we noticed, and we still have to take the origin labels on trust.

To build a clearer picture, we would need to taste many teas from both regions, across different producers and years, with age and storage kept as similar as we can. Repeating the tasting blind with other people would help us see which differences keep showing up.

Keep seller, producer label, year, season, portion size, and price close while preserving one Xishuangbanna and one
